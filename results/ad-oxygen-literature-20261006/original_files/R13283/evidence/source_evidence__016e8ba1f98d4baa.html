Microaeration of Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket reactor using desorption chamber gas: methane recovery and H2S removal

  • ,
  • ,
  • mail
    • SCIMAGO INSTITUTIONS RANKINGS

    ABSTRACT

    This study evaluated the integration between two technologies, a demonstration-scale desorption chamber (DC) and microaeration, in a demonstration-scale UASB reactor treating real domestic sewage, aiming to enhance methane recovery and reduce hydrogen sulfide. The DC removes dissolved CH₄ and H2S from liquid, generating a waste gas (containing CH₄, CO₂, H2S, and air - O₂ and N2), which was used in this study to microaerate the UASB reactor. The DC achieved median removal efficiencies of 55% for dissolved methane and 79% for dissolved hydrogen sulfide, despite the low gas-to-liquid ratio applied (rQ ≈ 0.042). Waste gas extracted from the DC was successfully reused as the microaeration source in the UASB reactor, resulting in a median 28% reduction in H₂S mass flow and a 60% decrease in biogas H₂S concentration, while also increasing methane mass flow by approximately 37%, even though the doses of O₂ and operational conditions varied substantially throughout the study. The integration of a desorption chamber and a microaerated UASB reactor produced biogas with a typical composition of ~50-54% CH₄ and ~800 ppmv H₂S, suitable for thermal energy recovery without any additional treatment or reducing the required treatment for energy recovery (biogas generator). The demonstration-scale conditions highlighted both the technical feasibility of the approach and the operational challenges of controlling microaeration under real wastewater variability, indicating a need for further research to standardize dosing strategies (L O2/L biogas instead of L O2/L effluent) to support full-scale implementation.

    Keywords:
    waste gas; biogas; energy recovery; odor nuisance; sulfide oxidation

    INTRODUCTION

    Anaerobic reactors, particularly the Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB) type, are widely used for sewage treatment in Brazil due to their operational simplicity, low sludge production, low energy consumption, and biogas generation, among other advantages. However, they also present limitations, including the need for post-treatment and challenges in managing biogas and dissolved gases, particularly methane (CH₄), a potent greenhouse gas, and hydrogen sulfide (H₂S), which is associated with odor, corrosion, and toxicity (Bressani-Ribeiro and Chernicharo, 2024).

    Dissolved methane (CH₄-D) has become a central topic in discussions regarding greenhouse gas emissions from wastewater treatment centers, especially in anaerobic systems. Unlike the methane recovered in the gaseous phase, this fraction remains in the liquid effluent. It may be gradually released into the atmosphere through desorption during transport or in downstream units within the WWTP. This release can increase overall emissions, as CH₄-D may account for 40% of the methane produced during treatment (Souza, Chernicharo and Aquino, 2011; Souza, Chernicharo and Melo, 2012). According to the IPCC (2019), methane emissions in anaerobic reactors are typically estimated based on the removed organic load, the maximum methane production potential (Bo), and the methane conversion factor (MCF), assuming that all methane is emitted as biogas. In this context, unaccounted CH₄-D may represent a significant source of fugitive emissions, especially when no removal, capture, or recovery strategies are in place, potentially affecting the facility’s GHG balance and decreasing the system’s energy efficiency.

    Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) is also a particularly problematic dissolved gas., A colorless, toxic, and corrosive compound, denser than air and partially soluble in water, it is characterized by a “rotten egg” odor and detectable by the human olfactory system at extremely low concentrations, on the order of 0.00047 ppmv (Metcalf and Eddy, 2016). As a result, it is a major contributor to operational issues in wastewater treatment plants, including odor nuisance, corrosion of concrete and metallic structures, damage to biogas energy recovery systems, occupational health risks, and the formation of sulfur oxides during gas combustion, which may contribute to acid rain. In anaerobic reactors, H₂S is predominantly present in the dissolved phase, accounting for 75.1% of the sulfide generated and corresponding to 57-64% of the influent sulfur. In contrast, the fraction present in the biogas is comparatively small, representing only 1.3% of the hydrogen sulfide formed and 0.9% of the influent sulfur (Souza, 2010; Souza, Chernicharo and Melo, 2012).

    One technique used to mitigate these issues is the desorption chamber (DC), which removes dissolved gases from anaerobic effluents (Glória et al., 2016; Huete et al., 2018). In this process, a large mass of CH₄ and H₂S is desorbed from the liquid phase and/or partially oxidized, generating a secondary gas stream, referred to as waste gas, which also contains significant amounts of air (N₂ and O₂).

    In this study, this waste gas, typically a residual stream requiring treatment, was repurposed as a source of microaeration for the UASB reactor, reintroducing the dissolved gases removed from the system. Microaeration, defined as the controlled introduction of small amounts of oxygen, has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance anaerobic reactor performance (Castro, Azevedo and Souza, 2021; Fu et al., 2023), as it can improve methane yield, reduce H₂S concentrations, and stabilize the process. However, challenges such as precise oxygen dosing, lack of standardization, and limited operational guidance still hinder large-scale implementation (Fu et al., 2023). Due to these challenges and to support the interpretation of some results of this study, one goal is to explore a more stable and efficiency-based parameter for microaeration.

    Another aspect is that the research on microaeration has largely focused on sludge and high-strength waste digesters, mainly at a laboratory or pilot scale. At the same time, few studies have explored its application to domestic sewage, particularly in full-scale UASB systems (Chen et al., 2020). In this context, the current study evaluated the integration of a desorption chamber with microaeration by applying the waste gas stream in a UASB reactor treating real domestic sewage, aiming to improve energy recovery, enhance system performance, and environmental outcomes.

    METHOD

    The experimental setup consisted of a demonstration-scale Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB) reactor, a desorption chamber (DC) filled with support media (3-inch Pall rings), a peristaltic pump, and tubular microbubble diffusers. The main parameters were listed in Table 1 as well as the dimensions of the system. Figure 1 illustrates a system sketch (a) and the real units (b).

    Figure 1 -

    The UASB reactor, which had been in operation for ten years prior to the start of the experiment, was fed with approximately 1.48 m³ h⁻¹ of domestic wastewater, resulting in a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of approximately 9.5 h and volumetric organic loading rate of 0.96 ± 0.55 kg COD m-3 day-1. The desorption chamber received the entire effluent from the UASB reactor, ensuring full system integration. In this unit, the average hydraulic loading rate (HLR) was 0.26 ± 0.04 m³ m⁻² min⁻¹, corresponding to applied flow rates of 0.41 ± 0.05 L s⁻¹. Gas extraction from the desorption chamber was performed using a Masterflex peristaltic pump with variable speed control (0-600 rpm).

    To evaluate the different microaeration application points and compare them with the conventional UASB reactor, three main phases were established. The study began with a control anaerobic phase (F0), during which the desorption chamber was monitored and adjusted for the subsequent use of waste gas for microaeration. This phase was characterized by low gas flow rates and, consequently, low gas-to-effluent flow ratios (rQ), with an average value of 0.042 ± 0.010. After this initial period, Phase F0 was applied at shorter intervals between transitions or whenever microaeration was interrupted due to unit maintenance (Figure 2). Consequently, the control anaerobic phase, later used as a reference for comparison with the microaerated reactor, was distributed chronologically throughout the entire experiment, resulting in mean values that were more representative and less influenced by seasonal factors (rainy periods, temperature variations, and water consumption habits).

    Subsequently, Phases 1 and 2 were implemented. These experimental phases involved evaluating different microaeration injection points within the UASB reactor. Two points of application were tested: in the headspace of the three-phase separator, approximately in the middle, and submerged in the water column of the three-phase separator, at a level between 0.5 and 1,0 m below the water surface. The experiment was divided into three experimental phases, as shown in Table 2. The desorption chamber observation followed the same microaeration phases to simplify analysis and comparisons. The experiment timeline can be found in Figure 2, focusing on the phase-change period.

    System performance monitoring encompassed a range of physical-chemical and gas-related parameters, using standard methods and instrumentation. Influent and effluent flow rates were measured directly in the field. Dissolved methane concentrations were determined using the headspace extraction method followed by gas chromatography, as described by Souza, Chernicharo and Aquino (2011). Dissolved hydrogen sulfide was quantified by the colorimetric method, according to Plas et al. (1992) and pH, temperature, and dissolved oxygen were monitored with a multiparameter probe (HACH® HQ40D). Biogas production was quantified using a gas meter (Ritter® TG3), while biogas composition required a portable gas analyzer equipped with electrochemical sensors (LANDTEC® GEM™ 5000). Statistical analyses were conducted using the non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test, with a 5% significance level.

    For the calculation of mass flow, flow rate data were normalized. A calculation of the biogas generated in the UASB reactor was carried out to estimate hydrogen sulfide removal efficiency and the increase in methane mass flow in the microaerated UASB reactor. The proper flow of biogas was calculated by subtracting the waste gas injected into the reactor from the total biogas with microaeration, while the composition was estimated from the average biogas composition obtained during the control anaerobic phase (F0).

    After the initial data analysis, an additional evaluation was conducted to better correlate the microaeration dosage with the observed process efficiencies. Due to the high variability observed in the data, both in terms of H₂S removal efficiency and methane mass flow, alternative dosing parameters were investigated to identify a more suitable performance indicator.

    In this context, different dosage parameters were evaluated based on previous studies (Krayzelova et al., 2015) and the previous experience of the researchers. The analyses were performed using Microsoft Excel® (Microsoft 365) and the tested dosing parameters were:

    • 1. the conventional microaeration rate expressed as liters of oxygen per liter of influent (LO2.Linf -1),

    • 2. liters of oxygen per liter of total biogas output (biogas produced plus waste gas used for microaeration) (LO2.Ltotal biogas 1), and

    • 3. liters of oxygen per liter of biogas produced in the UASB reactor (LO2.Lprod. biogas -1).

    Scatter plots with logarithmic trendlines were used to evaluate the most suitable dosing parameters. The analysis considered the coefficient of determination (R²) obtained for each relationship.

    RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

    Desorption Chamber Performance

    The experimental period in the desorption chamber lasted approximately 540 days, comprising phases with and without microaeration integration. Figure 3 presents the main results, with vertical dashed lines indicating the onset of microaeration (phases 1 and 2). The median removal efficiencies of dissolved methane and hydrogen sulfide were 55% and 79%, respectively, with maximum values of 78% and 99%, respectively.

    Figure 3 -
    a) Removal of dissolved CH₄ and H₂S (%) and methane concentration in the desorption chamber gas (% v/v) over the experimental days; b) Box plot of the variables presented.

    These medians were lower than those reported in previous studies: Glória et al. (2016) achieved a 60% methane and 84% hydrogen sulfide removal at a gas-to-liquid ratio (rQ) of 3.1, Huete et al. (2017) achieved methane removal efficiencies of up to 99% with rQ values between 0.5 and 1.0, while Santo et al. (2019) reported 89% and 86% removals, respectively, with an rQ of 0.1. In contrast, Santo et al. (2019) also used a rQ of 0.05 in a very similar desorption chamber, achieving 43% methane and 54% hydrogen sulfide removal, indicating that rQ plays an important role in removal effectiveness.

    The relatively lower efficiencies in the current study can be attributed to a group of aspects such as:

    • (1) the low average rQ applied (0.042), less than half the value used by Santo et al. (2019), which limited gas renewal at the interfaces and, consequently, mass transfer;

    • (2) the variability of the real domestic wastewater;

    • (3) higher hydraulic loading rate compared to Glória et al. (2016).

    As illustrated in Figure 3b, the removal efficiencies of dissolved CH₄ and H₂S (%) and the methane concentration in the desorption chamber gas (% v/v) fluctuated throughout the experimental period. Regarding the methane concentration, values generally range from 20 to 30%.

    Despite a few operational changes in the desorption chamber, establishing experimental phases was crucial to verify external influences and the relation between the DC and microaeration results. When the experimental phases are analyzed individually, clearer trends can be observed in the removal efficiencies of dissolved methane (CH₄) and dissolved hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) across the experimental phases (F0, F1, and F2), as presented in Figure 4. For CH₄ (Figure 4a), removal efficiencies predominantly ranged from 50 to 60%, with medians around 54 to 59% for all phases. Phases F0 and F1 exhibited lower data dispersion, indicating a more stable system behavior. In contrast, phase F2 showed greater variability, with minimum values near 20%, suggesting operational instability or a stronger influence of environmental factors on process performance.

    Figure 4 -
    Dissolved Gases Removal in Desorption Chamber.

    Regarding H₂S (Figure 4b), removal efficiencies were higher than those observed for CH₄, with medians of 75-80%. However, dispersion was more pronounced, especially in phase F1, where values ranged from 30 to 100%, approximately. In phases F0 and F2, although the minimum values were slightly higher, significant variability persisted, indicating that H₂S removal efficiency is more sensitive to operational conditions and system fluctuations.

    Figure 5 illustrates the concentrations of CH₄ (a) and H₂S (b) in the desorption chamber gas across the experimental phases F0, F1, and F2. For CH₄, median concentrations remained relatively stable across phases, ranging from approximately 21 to 26% v/v. Phase F2 exhibited the widest interquartile range, indicating greater variability in CH₄ concentration for the chamber during this phase. However, the minimum values were around 16-17% v/v across all phases.

    Figure 5 -
    Waste gas concentrations in Desorption Chamber, CH4 (a) and H2S (b).

    For H2S, phase F0 presented the lowest concentrations, with a median value of around 190 ppmv and a relatively narrow dispersion, indicating more stable conditions. In contrast, Phase F1 exhibited a substantial increase in H₂S levels, with a median concentration close to 500 ppmv and markedly higher variability, including maximum values near 1800 ppmv. Phase F2 showed intermediate behavior, with a median concentration of 380 ppmv and reduced dispersion compared to F1. Overall, the results indicate that H₂S concentrations in the waste gas were the lowest during F0 and most variable during F1, while F2 represented a partial recovery in stability.

    This behavior was also observed for methane during F1, confirming that this phase experienced greater operational and environmental instability. However, the median values were more comparable, particularly for F1 and F2, at approximately 500 and 350 ppm, respectively.Figure 6a shows the mass flow rate of CH₄ in the waste gas across the three experimental phases. The median values remained relatively stable, at around 200 g/d. The interquartile range and whiskers indicate high variability throughout the experimental period. In contrast, the mass flow rate of H₂S (Figure 6b) exhibited more pronounced variations, indicating more susceptibility due to operational and environmental changes. In Phase F0 (control), the H₂S mass flow rate remained at low levels, with a median value around 0.3 g/d and limited variability. In Phase F1, there was a marked increase in H₂S mass flow, with a median close to 1.0 g/d and higher dispersion. It is understood that the higher concentration of oxygen in the gas phase of DC at this phase (11%) may have created more difficulties for the partial oxidation of the sulfide to elemental sulfur, which requires lower oxygen concentrations in the liquid phase, than it promoted the complete oxidation of sulfide to sulfate, thus increasing the possibility of desorption to the gas phase. In Phase F2, the H₂S mass flow decreased compared to F1, with a median of approximately 0.7 g/d and reduced variability.

    Figure 6 -
    Mass Flow of CH4 (a) and H2S (b) in the waste gas.

    Performance of UASB Microaeration with Waste Gas

    The results presented in this section were obtained through the integration of DC waste gas and microaeration into the three-phase separator of the UASB reactor (phases 1 and 2, respectively). Figure 7a shows the H₂S mass flow (g d-1) removal efficiencies, which exhibited high variability in both phases. In terms of median values, both removal phases were very similar, at approximately 28%. These median efficiencies did not differ significantly (5% significance level, Mann-Whitney nonparametric test). Therefore, although the microaeration injection points were different, headspace (F1) and submerged (F2), no clear advantage was observed for the submerged application, despite the hypothesis that it would promote greater oxygen contact and direct microaeration in the liquid and scum layers.

    Figure 7 -
    Performance indicators of UASB microaeration in the experimental phases (F1 and F2): (a) H₂S mass flow removal (%); (b) CH4 mass flow increase (%).

    The removal efficiencies obtained were lower than those reported in other studies. In review studies of air-based microaeration, Krayzelova et al. (2015) reported removal efficiencies consistently above 72%, and Chen et al. (2020) observed a minimum efficiency of 52% in diverse anaerobic reactors. Castro, Azevedo and Souza (2021) concluded that air addition in the digestion zone of a UASB reactor treating domestic sewage showed hydrogen sulfide removal efficiencies from biogas greater than 90%, and Kraakman et at. (2023) reported removal efficiencies of at least 80% for H2S in large-scale, air microaerated wastewater municipal sludge digester biogas.

    An important factor contributing to this lower efficiency is the gas used in microaeration, which has a lower oxygen concentration than air (approximately 10%). In addition, the waste gas from the DC presents median hydrogen sulfide concentrations of around 190 ppmv (F0), 500 ppmv (F1), and 380 ppmv (F2), which also contribute to the H₂S content in the final biogas. Possibly the most important factor behind the understanding of the removal of H₂S around 30% is the residence time of the biogas in the headspace. This number accounted for a relatively low value of about 0.40 h, on average. Krayzelova et al. (2015), based on other studies, reported removal efficiencies that vary with residence time in the headspace, with removals of around 90% for residence times of 3-5 h and about 40% for residence times of approximately 1 h. However, achieving such high residence times in typical UASB reactors could result in substantial changes to the design parameters of the three-phase separators and an increase in associated costs.

    On the other hand, the approximately 28% efficiency represents an interesting, cost-effective removal, although relatively low, as the resulting biogas presented median concentrations of 820 ppmv. Compared to the typical H₂S concentration in biogas (2000 ppmv), the concentration reduction was achieved around 60%. Such a concentration (820 ppmv) directly meets the quality requirements for thermal energy recovery in boilers, without the need for additional treatment (< 1000 ppmv; Wellinger and Lindberg, 2001).

    As seen in Figure 7b, the relative increase in CH₄ mass flow (g d⁻¹) in the biogas was similar across operational phases F1 and F2, with a median of approximately 37% and considerable variability in the data. Statistical analysis indicated no significant differences between the two phases at the 5% significance level (Mann-Whitney test). Therefore, submerging the injection point for the DC waste gas did not provide an observable advantage in methane recovery.

    The increase in methane mass flow was estimated through a mass balance approach. The total CH₄ mass flow measured in the mixed gas stream was compared with the expected CH₄ mass flow assuming that no DC waste gas was introduced. This reference condition was calculated considering the normalized total volumetric flow minus the normalized volumetric flow of waste gas, and assuming that the CH₄ concentration in the resulting flow would correspond to the average CH₄ concentration measured in the anaerobic phase.

    Based on this calculation procedure, the observed increase in CH₄ mass flow is likely associated with the recovery of dissolved methane by the DC unit. In contrast, the possibility that microaeration occurring in the upper region of the three-phase separator significantly enhanced anaerobic biodegradation is considered low (Castro, Azevedo and Souza, 2021). Previous studies have shown that dissolved methane losses in the effluent of UASB reactors may reach 60% of the methane usually captured in the biogas (Souza, Chernicharo and Aquino, 2011).

    From an energy recovery perspective, the 37% increase represents a potentially valuable gain in methane. Although some dilution of methane occurred due to mixing between biogas and DC waste gas, the resulting CH₄ concentration was around 54%, indicating that the dilution was not pronounced. The waste gas stream still contained relevant concentrations of recovered CH₄ and CO₂, approximately 22 and 5%, respectively. Moreover, methane concentrations around 54% remain suitable for most thermal and electrical energy conversion systems operating with biogas (Sun et al., 2015; Wellinger and Lindberg, 2001).

    Concerning the point of oxygen/gas application, submerged microaeration presented disadvantages compared to headspace injection. These included a trend toward lower removal efficiency, likely due to oxygen consumption in the liquid phase, as well as operational challenges (e.g., diffuser inspection and replacement) and increased system complexity. Supporting the preference for headspace application, most sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB) are predominantly found in the headspace, indicating that sulfide oxidation is primarily occurring there (Krayzelova et al., 2015).

    Study for a more suitable parameter for microaeration dosage

    Due to the high variability observed in the data, both in H2S removal efficiency and in methane mass flow, alternative parameters were evaluated to better describe the relationship between microaeration dosage and process performance.

    The coefficients of determination (R²) obtained between the evaluated microaeration parameters and the process responses (hydrogen sulfide removal and methane mass flow increase) are summarized as follows:

    • (1) liters of oxygen per liter of influent ( LO2.Linf -1) resulted in R² values equal to zero for both variables;

    • (2) liters of oxygen per liter of total biogas output (LO2.Ltotal biogas -1) resulted in R² values of 0.46 for hydrogen sulfide removal and 0.23 for methane mass flow increase; and

    • (3) liters of oxygen per liter of biogas produced in the UASB reactor (LO2.Lprod. biogas -1) resulted in R² values of 0.60 and 0.41 for hydrogen sulfide removal and methane mass flow increase, respectively.

    Therefore, the microaeration rate expressed relative to the biogas produced in the UASB reactor (LO2. Lprod. biogas -1) showed the strongest correlations with the evaluated performance indicators. This parameter excludes the volumetric contribution of the DC gas used for microaeration, thereby better representing the operational conditions of the reactor.

    Considering this newly defined parameter, the microaeration rates obtained for operational phases F1 and F2 (Figure 8) were compared. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two phases (5% significance level, Mann-Whitney test). Therefore, data from both phases were grouped for the subsequent correlation analyses.

    Figure 8 -
    Microaeration dosages in the experimental phases of the UASB/DC system (LO2.Lbiog -1).

    From the graphs in Figure 9 (a and b), it was observed that the logarithmic correlations (R2) indicate moderate explanatory power (0.60 and 0.41, respectively) for H2S removal and CH4 increase, respectively, and were much superior to the other tested options. Therefore, in the 0.070 and 0.240 LO2.Lprod. biog -1 range, increasing the microaeration rate promotes an increase in H2S removal and CH4 production gain. However, caution is required not to dilute the biogas in methane beyond the useful range for energy recovery. Thus, based on the correlation graphs, it seems more appropriate to work with rates between 0.140 and 0.180 LO2.Lprod. biog -1 to achieve approximately 40% CH4 increase and 30% H2S removal. As mentioned previously, the expected concentrations are around 50% CH4 and 800 ppm H2S in the resulting biogas for combustion and energy utilization.

    Figure 9 -
    Relationship between UASB microaeration rate and (a) H2S mass flows removal and (b) CH4 mass flow increase in biogas.

    Thus, the most appropriate oxygen dosage, based on the waste gas in relation to the biogas produced by the UASB reactor, would be around 16% (0.140 to 0.180 L O₂·L⁻¹ produced biogas). Compared with the range reported by Krayzelová et al. (2015), 0.3-3%, this value appears quite high. However, within the specific context of a UASB reactor treating mainly domestic wastewater, with short residency time in the headspace, it is proved to be the best possible scenario for H₂S removal, from both the biogas and the DC waste gas, while also allowing the recovery of dissolved methane without compromising the biogas through excessive dilution.

    Considering this perspective, based on the results and the simplicity and economy of the system, which involves units, equipment, and piping without considerable complexity, it can be concluded that complementing a UASB reactor with a DC system integrating its waste gas to promote microeration inside the three-phase separator constitutes a viable technical and economic option to be developed, especially when applied in the headspace, recovering dissolved methane along with the biogas.

    Oxygen and methane concentrations for operational safety of the UASB/DC system

    To discuss safety aspects related to microaerated biogas in the UASB reactor and to waste gas in the DC, the respective graphs (Figures 10a and 10b) show oxygen and methane concentrations over the experimental period (time counted from the onset of microaeration using waste gas, Phase 1). The operating conditions of the gas lines (biogas and waste gas) were: ambient temperature (average around 25°C) and pressure around atmospheric pressure (1 atm); therefore, the safety parameters used below refer to conditions close to these standards, obtained from the flammability diagram between CH4, O2, and N2.

    Figure 10 -
    Time series of O2 and CH4 concentration results obtained from the integration of the UASB/DC system: (a) biogas from the microaerated UASB reactor using waste gas, and (b) waste gas from the DC directed to the UASB reactor.

    In the microaerated UASB reactor (Figure 10a), O₂ concentrations always remained below 8%, well under the minimum oxygen concentration (MOC) of 12%. This value represents the threshold which combustion cannot occur due to the lack of oxidant/oxygen. Additionally, CH₄ concentrations were much higher than the upper flammability limit (UFL) of 15% in air, a value above which combustion cannot occur due to excess fuel/methane (Crowl, 2023; Guo et al., 2024; Mashuga and Crowl, 1998).

    For the waste gas in the DC under operation (Figure 10b), O₂ concentrations were expectedly higher, but with median and mean values close to 10%, and thus frequently (over 75% of the data) below the MOC of 12%. O₂ concentrations approached 12% on only three days, and on two additional days they presented values of approximately 13-14%. On the other hand, even when concentrations were close to or slightly above the MOC (12-14%), the minimum CH₄ concentration required for the mixture to be flammable would be within much more restrictive and reduced ranges, approximately 6-10% CH₄ (Crowl, 2003; Mashuga and Crowl, 1998). Methane concentrations in the waste gas were always above 16%, with mean and median values close to 24%.

    The 12% MOC value is specifically applied to contexts of methane dilution with nitrogen (N2) and air. When CO2 is present, this limit can rise to 14.5% (Crowl, 2003), making the safety range less restrictive. In the current study, biogas and waste gas had average CO2 concentrations of 6 and 5%, respectively, along with high N2 levels of 35 and 61%, respectively. Due to this composition, the 12% O2 limit is a more conservative and therefore safer approach. Additionally, an extra safety margin is recommended, establishing the operational limit at 10% O2 (Crowl, 2003). Therefore, in future studies and applications, it is recommended to adjust the microaeration rate considering this oxygen limit (10%) in the waste gas as well.

    Therefore, under normal operation at these concentration levels, safety risks are considered low, especially for the biogas in the UASB reactor. Under transient operational conditions, it is necessary to plan and implement conscious safety and precautionary measures. Continuous O₂ monitoring in the DC waste gas and occasional application in the UASB biogas are recommended for operational monitoring and control of the system.

    CONCLUSION

    The results of this study demonstrate that integrating a desorption chamber with microaeration into a UASB reactor is a promising strategy to mitigate dissolved gases, achieving relevant removals of CH₄ and H₂S with low energy demand and simple operation. The use of waste gas as a source of microaeration proved technically feasible. Regarding the injection point, no major differences were observed between the headspace and submerged applications.

    This study achieved performance close to, and even exceeding, a 37% increase in CH4 in the biogas mass flow and a 28% H2S removal in the mixed biogas and waste gas mass flows. As a characterization of the mixed and treated flow, it was possible to reach concentrations of approximately 50% CH4 and 800 ppm H2S, which can be used in various contexts as renewable energy.

    As this study was conducted in a demonstration-scale unit treating real domestic wastewater under practical conditions, the findings provide valuable insights into the limitations and the potential of this integrated approach. Nevertheless, challenges remain with oxygen dosing control and the operational variability inherent to full-scale plants, including fluctuations in influent characteristics, flow rate, and biogas production. These results highlight the need for further investigation to establish robust operational guidelines and to advance the scalability of microaeration in anaerobic sewage treatment systems. Future studies should also evaluate alternative control parameters for microaeration, such as the L O₂/L biogas ratio.
