Nondestructive and three-dimensional visualization by identifying elements using synchrotron radiation microscale X-ray CT reveals microbial and cavity distributions in anaerobic granular sludge

ABSTRACT

We developed a nondestructive three-dimensional microbial visualization method utilizing synchrotron radiation X-ray microscale computed tomography to better understand the relationship between microorganisms and their surrounding habitats. The method was tested and optimized using a mixture of axenic Escherichia coli and Comamonas testosteroni. The osmium-thiocarbohydrazide-osmium method was used to stain all the microbial cells, and gold in situ hybridization was used to detect specific phylogenetic microbial groups. The stained samples were embedded in epoxy resin for microtomographic analysis. Differences in X-ray absorbances were calculated by subtracting the pre-L3-edge images from the post-L3-edge images to visualize the osmium and gold signals. Although we successfully detected cells stained with osmium, those labeled with gold were not detected, probably because of the insufficient density of gold atoms in the microbial cells. We then applied the developed technique to anaerobic granules and visualized the distribution of microbial cells and extracellular polymeric substances. Empty spaces were highlighted to determine the cavity distribution in granules. Numerous independent cavities of different sizes were identified in the granules. The developed method can be applied to various environmental samples for deeper insights into microbial life in their habitats.

IMPORTANCE

Microorganisms inhabit diverse environments and often form biofilms. One factor that affects their community structure is the surrounding physical environment. The arrangement of residential space within the formed biofilm plays a crucial role in the supply and transportation of substances, as well as the discharge of metabolites. Conventional approaches, such as scanning electron microscopy and confocal laser scanning microscopy combined with fluorescence in situ hybridization, have limitations as they provide information primarily from the biofilm surface and cross-sections. In this study, we developed a method for detecting microorganisms in biofilms using synchrotron radiation X-ray microscale computer tomography. The developed method allows nondestructive three-dimensional observation of biofilms at a single-cell resolution (voxel size of approximately 200 nm), facilitating an understanding of the relationship between microorganisms and their physical habitats.

KEYWORDS: synchrotron radiation X-ray computer tomography, SPring-8, L3-edge energy subtraction, anaerobic granular sludge, cavity

INTRODUCTION

Microorganisms inhabiting diverse environments, including the sea, lakes, soil, sediments, plants, rhizospheres, and wastewater treatment sludge (1), often form biofilms. Microbial community structures in biofilms are influenced by their surrounding physical environment (2), in addition to the supply of chemical materials (organic and inorganic) and biological partnerships with other organisms [e.g., symbiosis (3), parasitism (4), and quorum sensing (5)]. The arrangement of residential space within the formed biofilm plays a crucial role in the supply and transportation of substances, as well as the discharge of metabolites (6, 7). Microorganism distribution in biofilms has been extensively studied (8, 9); however, only a few studies have examined the physical spaces within biofilms (7, 10, 11). Integrated spatial observation of all biofilm components, including the cavity space, is necessary for comprehensive understanding of the biofilm formation, growth, maintenance, and calving processes. However, the frequently used scanning electron microscopy (SEM) technique only provides information on the surface or cross-sections of biofilms and often lacks spatial information.

The upflow anaerobic sludge blanket (UASB) process is one of the most successful processes in anaerobic wastewater treatment (12). Wastewater is introduced from the bottom of the reactor while treated water is discharged from the top. Wastewater is treated using microorganisms in granules formed in the sludge bed. Granular sludge is an aggregate of high-density microorganisms and can vary in size by several millimeters. This size depends on factors such as the wastewater type, upflow velocity, and reactor structure. Stable retention of granular sludge is vital for maintaining wastewater treatment processes. Therefore, the physical and biological structures of granules and granulation processes have been extensively investigated (13, 14).

Details regarding the physical structures of anaerobic granules, including cavities, channels, pores, and craters, are essential for understanding the metabolism and transport of materials within the granules (7). These cavities and channels are thought to facilitate the transport of nutrients and metabolites. Many cavities are commonly observed in granules by SEM observations (10, 15). Channels are considered bridges between cavities and form a cascade structure with fine channels evolving into larger channels (16); these are essential for effectively releasing biogas from granules. These physical structures are considered to be related to microbial metabolism. The biological structures of anaerobic granules are routinely investigated using SEM and confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) combined with fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) (8, 9). Applying a layered model of granules, coupled with the biological structure information obtained using CLSM-FISH, enables prediction of the metabolic processes in specific microbial groups (17). However, CLSM-FISH can only provide two-dimensional or partial three-dimensional information (with a depth of ~100 µm).

Essential information, such as the distribution of specific microorganisms throughout the granule, spatial arrangement of cavities, and connectivity of channels remains lacking and is needed for gaining further insight into the structure of granules, encompassing microbial metabolism and material transport. Nondestructive and three-dimensional observations of granules, encompassing their surface and internal structure, are crucial for obtaining such data. X-ray computed tomography (CT) is a promising approach for this purpose (18, 19). However, conventional X-ray CT systems face challenges in identifying specifically stained microorganisms and may lack sufficient spatial resolution for detection at the single-cell level.

To address these challenges, we focused on X-ray microscale CT (X-ray µCT) at the super photon ring beamline 47XU (SPring-8 BL47XU), one of the most powerful synchrotron radiation facilities. Synchrotron radiation X-ray µCT (SR X-ray µCT) offers tomography with high spatial resolution, utilizing high-energy and high-brightness X-ray energy (20). SR X-ray µCT can employ a bright monochromatic X-ray, whereas general X-ray CT only allows the use of white X-rays from X-ray tubes. This distinction enables identification of specific elements by recognizing absorption edge energies (21, 22). Researchers leveraging these advantages have successfully applied SR X-ray µCT to various samples (23–25).

In this study, we attempted to develop a nondestructive and three-dimensional visualization method for microorganisms in granular sludge using SR X-ray µCT to clarify their physical structures (i.e., cavities and channels) and biological structures (i.e., microbial distribution). Considering the X-ray energy range of SPring-8 BL47XU and the existing microbial staining methods, two elements, i.e., osmium and gold, were selected: osmium for staining all microbial cells using the osmium-thiocarbohydrazide-osmium (OTO) method (24, 26) and gold for phylogenetic staining using gold in situ hybridization (ISH) (27).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Detection of microbial cells stained with osmium and gold by SR X-ray μCT

An artificial mixture of Comamonas testosteroni and Escherichia coli was prepared to assess the detectability of microbial cells labeled with osmium and gold through SR X-ray μCT scans. Embedding the samples with epoxy resin prevented drift during CT imaging. The OTO method was employed to stain all microbial cells in the artificial mixture with osmium, given its well-established ability to stain unsaturated C with a high concentration of osmium (24, 26). Osmium-derived signals were identified by subtracting the pre-edge images from the post-edge images, creating images that illustrated differences in X-ray absorbance (Fig. 1 and 2A). Numerous osmium-derived signals were evident in the area where the artificial mixture was applied, whereas no signals were observed where the cells were absent. The average signal-to-noise ratio calculated based on the brightness distribution (Fig. 2C) was 3.5 (standard deviation 0.23), which was comparable with that obtained after 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole staining (2.0–7.5) (28). Consistent results were obtained across all the reconstructed layers. This result indicates that a sufficient amount of osmium was introduced into the microbial cells using the OTO method, allowing detectable differences in X-ray absorbance between pre- and post-edge images. Although a previous study detected osmium-stained organic matter within soil aggregate structures using the SR X-ray μCT and L3-edge subtraction method (24), this is the first report of osmium-stained microbial cell detection using SR X-ray μCT.

Gold-labeled cells were detected using the same approach. The specific labeling of gold on C. testosteroni in the artificial mixture by gold-ISH and subsequent gold enhancement were initially confirmed by light microscopy and SEM-energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) (Fig. S1A and B). On CT imaging, there was no detectable difference in the X-ray absorbance at any area of the images taken at the pre- and post-edge energies of gold L3 (Fig. 2B and C). An attempt to detect signals by dividing the X-ray absorbances (post-edge X-ray absorbance/pre-edge X-ray absorbance) also showed no difference. Gold-derived signals were obtained from a gold material (1.6-µm Gold Microcarriers; Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) used as a control, suggesting the following two possibilities: labeled gold was lost during the sample preparation process, or the amount of gold-labeled on microbial cells was insufficient to provide a good signal-to-noise ratio. The same samples embedded in resin were analyzed using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) to successfully detect gold signals (Fig. S1C). These results suggest that the gold labeling was successful. However, the amount of gold was insufficient to produce a detectable difference in the X-ray absorbance between the pre- and post-edge images in this study. Pure culture samples were fixed in exponential growth phases, and intense fluorescence signals were obtained after standard FISH, indicating that the amount of rRNA in the microbial cells was high. Therefore, microbial cells should be labeled with more gold. We used the recently developed hemin-tyramide signal amplification (TSA) method to label cells using gold (28), but the gold signals were still undetected. Although prolonging the incubation time for gold enhancement by autometallography could result in increased gold deposition, this often leads to nonspecific and undesired gold deposition. Because our protocol was optimized to maximize the gold enhancement process, further elongation of the incubation time was not recommended. An alternative approach to enhance gold signals is the use of polynucleotide probes instead of oligonucleotide probes (29). Polynucleotide probes have higher sensitivity but lower specificity for nucleic acid sequencing discrimination. However, they are still useful for identifying higher taxonomic groups with increased signal-to-noise ratios (30). Another option is to use the K-edge of gold (approximately 80.7 keV) (25, 31). However, this energy is beyond the range of the available X-ray energy at SPring-8 BL47XU.

Three-dimensional internal structure of granular sludge

Based on the above results, the OTO method was applied to stain microorganisms with osmium in the granules. Notably, granular sludge contains extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), which are also stained with osmium using the OTO method. Consequently, the obtained signals were considered to originate from both microorganisms and EPS. Osmium signals were acquired from granule samples without drifting during SR X-ray µCT scans, allowing for the successful visualization of the spatial distribution of microorganisms in granular sludge (Fig. 3A). Using these data, we identified areas lacking microorganisms and EPS in the granules, representing potential cavities and channels, as well as the presence of inorganic substances. These findings provide valuable insights into the physical structure of granules. Although the localization of osmium was specifically identified in this study, the compositional information of other materials could not be identified because the absorption edge energies of other elements were outside the range of SR X-ray µCT. This included the cavity area in the CT images. Because CT images represent the absorption of source X-rays, it is challenging to determine whether the cavity spaces are filled with water, gas, or any other filling material composed of light elements. The granules were observed using SEM, and the original CT images were carefully checked to confirm the presence of numerous cavities but not mineral-like materials. Furthermore, thin sections of the granules were stained with SYBR Gold and observed using CLSM, confirming the presence of cavities (Fig. S2).

For a detailed analysis of the cavities, the color gradation of the images was inverted. Consequently, numerous independent cavities were identified (Fig. 3B, Fig. S3A; Videos S1 and S2). Thin cylinders were extracted from the sludge surface to the core, and the cavity sizes and their proportions in each section were analyzed using ImageJ (Fiji) (n = 3). These values, along with the granule depths, are presented in Fig. 3C and D and in Fig. S3B and C, revealing an average size of 3.8 (±0.67)–42 (±8.7) µm² and a proportion of 0.29% (±0.11%)–12% (±4.2%) (standard deviation in parenthesis). Granule cavities and channels are believed to facilitate the transport of nutrients and metabolites and contribute to the release of biogas produced within the granules (32). However, channels connecting the cavities were not observed in this study. Previous research has reported various channel diameters [ranging from 3 nm to 364 µm and 99.85% of the total channel volume comprises channels with a diameter of 37.7 nm or less (7)], with most channels being undetectable even with the voxel size used in this study (i.e., 795.2 nm for the three-dimensional viewer and 198.8 nm for the original data). Although the SPring-8 BL47XU is capable of nanoscale CT scanning with a voxel size of approximately 50 nm, visualizing channels in the granules remains challenging.

One of the observed granules contained a large cavity in the core part. The activity of microorganisms in the central part of the granules is considered to decrease because of the limited availability of substrates compared with that near the surface. FISH-CLSM observations of sectioned granular sludge samples usually show a lack of fluorescent signals from the center of the granule, suggesting that the microorganisms do not have sufficient rRNA for detection (i.e., low activity) or the absence of microorganisms (6, 8). The results revealed no microorganisms at the core of the granules and that the granules had a cavity that potentially influenced their settleability. Biogas retention in the central cavity may lead to flotation and bulking. The three-dimensional structure of granule cavities can provide insights into granule formation, growth, and calving, which are crucial for effective anaerobic sludge management.

Significance and perspective of the application of SR X-ray μCT

Commercially available CT systems enable three-dimensional visualization of various samples. Contrast agents are sometimes used for observation in CT systems (33–35), but generally, staining is not mandatory. Accessing instruments and obtaining three-dimensional images are easier. However, identification of these elements is challenging. A combination of other methods was employed to obtain elemental information for the samples. One study analyzed the brightness distribution of CT images to extract peaks corresponding to specific elements (36). Alternatively, data obtained using other instruments, such as element identification using SEM-EDX (37), have been combined.

Furthermore, the method developed in this study enabled us to obtain three-dimensional images and simultaneously identify the elements. SR X-ray µCT provides high spatial resolution, enabling analysis at the single-cell level. SR-X-ray µCT also holds the potential to visualize the three-dimensional spatial distribution of specific groups of microorganisms, even though technical improvements for phylogenetically detecting microorganisms are essential.

Conclusion

In this study, we successfully developed a method for detecting osmium-stained microorganisms using X-ray µCT at SPring-8 BL47XU. However, we were unable to detect microorganisms stained with gold-ISH using this method. The developed technique allowed us to observe the distribution of microorganisms within anaerobic sludge granules. It also helped us identify the presence, distribution, and size of cavities, providing deeper insights into nutrient and metabolite transportation, as well as granule formation, growth, and calving. This approach can be utilized to study high-density microbial aggregates, such as granules and microorganisms, in various environments, thereby offering fundamental insights into the relationships between microorganisms and their physical habitats.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample preparation

A mixture of pure strains of Comamonas testosteroni (NBRC14951) and Escherichia coli (ATCC700926) was used. The strains were cultivated in Luria-Bertani medium at 37°C with agitation. The cells were harvested during the exponential growth phase. Anaerobic granular sludge was sampled from a UASB reactor used for treating food industry wastewater. The pure cultures and granular sludge sample were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution for 12 h at 4°C and stored in an ethanol/phosphate-buffered saline (PBS: 140-mM NaCl, 20-mM PO43− [pH 7.4]) solution at −20°C.

Gold-ISH

Fixed samples of an artificial mixture of C. testosteroni and E. coli (at a ratio of 1:1) and granular sludge samples were subjected to gold-ISH to label cells phylogenetically with gold. All experimental procedures were conducted in test tubes. The solutions used in each step were removed by centrifugation (for the artificial mixture unless otherwise indicated) or precipitation (for the granular sludge samples). The protocol was optimized based on gold-FISH (27). The fixed samples were initially treated with lysozyme [10 mg/mL in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 7.5), 37°C, 30 min] prior to gold-ISH for better penetration of larger molecules [i.e., horseradish peroxidase (HRP) and streptavidin]. The samples were then washed in PBS containing 0.05% Triton X-100 (PBSX) (for 30 min at room temperature) and then in PBS (twice for 10 min at room temperature).

For in situ hybridization, the samples were mixed with hybridization buffer [20-mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 0.9-M NaCl, 35% (vol/vol) formamide, 1.0% (wt/vol) blocking reagent (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany), 10% dextran sulfate, and 0.01% (wt/vol) SDS] containing 0.1-µM BET42a HRP-labeled probes [targeting Betaproteobacteria (5′-GCC TTC CCA CTT CGT TT-3′)] (38). When using BET42a, an unlabeled competitor probe [sequence identical to GAM42a targeting Gammaproteobacteria (5′-GCC TTC CCA CAT CGT TT-3′)] was also added to the hybridization buffer. After incubation at 40°C overnight, the samples were washed in buffer [20-mM Tris-HCl (pH 7.5), 80-mM NaCl, and 0.01% (wt/vol) SDS] at 42°C for 30 min.

For TSA, the samples were mixed with PBSX for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the TSA reaction was conducted in a working buffer (a mixture of 0.25 vol of 40% dextran sulfate, 0.01 vol of 10% blocking reagent, and 0.75 vol of buffer provided in the kit) containing tyramide-biotin (the dilution ratio, 1:50) (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA) for 60 min at room temperature. After the reaction, samples were washed twice with PBSX. For the immunological reaction, the samples were initially incubated in 1% (wt/vol) bovine serum albumin (BSA; FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Solution, Osaka, Japan) dissolved in PBS for 30 min at room temperature. Then, the same amount of a mixture containing Nanogold-Streptavidin [diluted 1:50 with 1% (wt/vol) BSA in PBS; Nanoprobes, Yaphank, NY, USA] was added to the pre-incubated mixture (final dilution ratio of Nanogold-Streptavidin, 1:100), and the samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After incubation, the samples were washed once with PBSX for 30 min, followed by washing twice with ultrapure water for 10 min each.

Gold enhancement (gold-based autometallography) was performed by incubating the samples with GoldEnhance EM Plus (Nanoprobes) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Samples were incubated in the dark for 30 min at room temperature and washed twice with ultrapure water for 10 min each.

Osmium staining

The artificial mixture of cells was mixed with 2% (wt/vol) agar and solidified in a micropipette tip by cooling on ice. The agar-embedded cells and sludge granules (hereafter “samples”) were subjected to glutaraldehyde fixation and osmium staining using the OTO method, according to previous reports with some modifications (24). First, the samples were fixed in a 3.1% (wt/vol) glutaraldehyde solution (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Solution) diluted in ultrapure water for 3 h at 4°C with shaking. The samples were then immersed in pre-chilled ultrapure water for 15 min at 4°C. After repeating the washing with ultrapure water six times (15 min, 4°C), the samples were immersed in a solution of 2% (wt/vol) osmium tetroxide (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Solution) dissolved in ultrapure water for 3 h at 4°C with shaking. The samples were washed six times with ultrapure water, then immersed in 1% (wt/vol) thiocarbohydrazide (Tokyo Chemical Industry, Tokyo, Japan) for 3 h at 4°C. After the samples were washed six times for 15 min each with ultrapure water at 4°C, they were again immersed in 2% (wt/vol) osmium tetroxide for 3 h at 4°C with shaking and washed six times for 15 min each with ultrapure water at 4°C.

Epoxy resin embedding

The samples were embedded in epoxy resin after osmium staining according to previous reports, with some modifications (24, 39). The samples were first dehydrated with an ethanol series [30%, 50%, 70%, and 80% (vol/vol) ethanol for 30 min each at 4°C; 90% and 95% ethanol for 30 min each at room temperature; and ≥99.5% ethanol for 30 min at room temperature]. Ethanol with a concentration of ≥99.5% was prepared by adding a molecular sieve (3A 1/16, FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Solution) to 99.5% ethanol. Dehydration in ≥99.5% ethanol was repeated thrice. Subsequently, the samples were embedded in an epoxy resin after osmium staining. The samples were first immersed in n-butyl glycidyl ether (QY-1; Nissin EM Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) for 20 min, repeated three times, and left overnight. The samples were then immersed in 25%, 33%, 50%, 67%, 75%, and 100% (vol/vol) Petropoxy 154 resin (Burnham Petrographics LLC, Rathdrum, ID, USA) diluted in QY-1 for 2, 4, 6, 12, 24, and 72 h, respectively, at room temperature. The resin was de-aerated in vacuo before use to prevent air bubble contamination. The samples were placed in a mold filled with 100% Petropoxy 154 and heated in an oven at 140°C for 20 min for polymerization of the epoxy resin. After cooling to room temperature, the resin-embedded samples were cut and sharpened (Fig. 4).

SR X-ray μCT scanning and image analysis

X-ray CT was performed using BL47XU (20) at SPring-8 (Koto, Hyogo, Japan). The X-ray energies were adjusted at 10.86 and 10.87 keV, which are the pre- and post-edge energies of osmium L3, respectively. For gold, 11.91 and 11.92 keV were used as the pre- and post-edge energies of gold L3. For all samples, 1,800 projection images were acquired with an exposure time of 200 ms. The acquired CT images were reconstructed using the filter-back reconstruction method with 4,096 × 4,096 pixels (pixel size 0.1988 µm). The projection images were not aligned before the tomographic reconstruction because the rotation stage for the sample achieved a wobble size of less than 100 nm at SPring-8 BL47XU (40). The software and manuals used for computed tomography analysis can be downloaded from http://www-bl20.spring8.or.jp/xct/index-e.html. Image J (Fiji) (41) was used for all CT image analyses. The subtracted images were converted into 8-bit images. The color gradation was reversed to highlight cavities, and the peaks corresponding to the cavities were extracted. Cavity area percentage and average size were analyzed using the “analyze particle” function in Image J (Fiji).

XRF analysis

The resin-embedded samples used for SR X-ray μCT scanning were analyzed using an X-ray fluorescence analyzer (XGT-5000; HORIBA, Kyoto, Japan). The voltages were set to 50 kV (2.03–2.22 keV) and 30 kV (7.83–13.38 keV). The duration of the experiment was 16 min.

SEM observation

For SEM, samples were subjected to glutaraldehyde fixation, dehydration, and freeze-drying in t-butyl ethanol. Initially, the samples were fixed with 2.5% (vol/vol) glutaraldehyde and washed twice with ultrapure water. The samples were then dehydrated using an ethanol series (25%, 50%, and 75% ethanol diluted in ultrapure water for 5 min each, followed by 99.5% ethanol three times for 5 min each) and a t-butyl ethanol series (25%, 50%, and 75% t-butyl ethanol diluted in ethanol for 5 min each, followed by 99.9% t-butyl ethanol three times for 5 min each). Finally, the samples were lyophilized in vacuo for 6 h. A JSM-6500F microscope (JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) was used for the observation. The secondary electron images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV (5 nA). Elemental analysis was performed using by SEM-EDX (JED-2200; JEOL Ltd.).

Fluorescence observation of granular sludge

Thin sections of granular sludge samples were prepared and microscopically observed as described in a previous study (9). In brief, the samples were immersed in an optimum cutting temperature compound (Sakura Finetec, Torrance, CA, USA), frozen at −20°C, and sectioned to 30-µm thickness using a cryostat microtome (CM3050S; Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sectioned sludge was stained with SYBR Gold (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for 10 min and subsequently immersed in ultrapure water for 1 min at room temperature. Microscopic observations were performed using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM710; Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany).
