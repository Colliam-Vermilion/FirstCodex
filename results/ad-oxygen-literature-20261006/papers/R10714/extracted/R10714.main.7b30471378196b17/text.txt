This study explored the efficiency of CO2-, N2-, and H2- nanobubble treatment in anaerobic 
13 
digestion (AD) of rice straw, with a focus on the processes and metabolic pathways of 
14 
hydrolytic acidification, and revealed the underlying mechanisms. Mechanistic investigations 
15 
revealed that nanobubbles, particularly CO2 nanobubbles, significantly increased the 
16 
degradation of amorphous cellulose, resulting in higher levels of soluble carbohydrates (6.27% 
17 
– 11.13%), VFAs (4.39% – 24.50%), and a remarkable cumulative H2 yield (74 – 94 times) 
18 
Microbial community analysis indicated that the CO2 nanobubble promoted the growth of 
19 
acidifying bacterial communities, such as Mobilitalea, unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, and 
20 
Bacteroides. This indicates that the introduction of CO2 nanobubbles improved the total 
21 
abundance of predicted functional enzymes were increased by 14%, resulting in the 
22 
production of more easily degradable intermediates. Based on the analysis of total methane 
23 
production and kinetic analysis, it can be concluded that nanobubble addition enhanced 
24 
methane production levels of 4.22% - 7.79% with lower lag time (λ) (0.88–1.06 day) compared 
25 
to the control group (1.09 day). The results also elucidated changes in relative enzymatic 
26 
activities involved in the bioconversion of cellulose and hemicellulose during the hydrolysis 
27 
stage with nanobubble treatment. This work is more beneficial for understanding the 
28 
promoting effect and mechanism of nanobubbles on AD, facilitating the more precise 
29 
application of nanobubble technology in the field of renewable energy.

Rice straw, a lignocellulosic biomass, exhibits a complex fibre structure resulting from the 
44 
intricate cross-linking of lignocellulose into highly polymerized crystals [7]. Lignocellulosic 
45 
biomass exhibits lower biodegradability compared with other organics such as carbohydrates. 
46 
Therefore, the CH4 yield from the AD of lignocellulosic biomass accounts for less 60% of its 
47 
theoretical potential [8]. As known, the hydrolysis process, which is the initial step in the AD 
48 
process, imposes constraints on the conversion of lignocellulosic materials into desirable end 
49 
products [8]. Researchers have made several efforts to enhance the AD performance of rice 
50 
straw, including pre-treating the materials to disrupt the lignocellulosic structure [9], microbial 
51 
augmentation by adding specific bacterial cultures during the AD process [10], and co-
52 
digestion of rice straw with manure feedstocks [11]. However, the shortcomings of these 
53 
approaches become evident when they are applied. For example, potentially hazardous 
54 
byproducts, including 5-hydroxymethylfurfural, furfural, and phenolic compounds, may arise 
55 
following acid pretreatment, particularly under elevated temperatures [12]. In order to avoid 
56 
secondary pollution, certain adsorption methods or adsorbents are also needed [13]. 
57 
Improper handling of adsorbents can also cause pollution and increase treatment costs. Hence, 
58 
developing a novel intensification approach that requires fewer chemical additives and proves 
59 
to be more cost-effective and efficient is vital.

[{"type": "pdf_block", "pdf_page": 6, "pdf_page_label": "", "block": 9, "bbox_points": [36.36000061035156, 387.2879638671875, 527.11328125, 708.384033203125]}]
Various methods, including ethanol–water exchange, temperature change, electrochemical 
183 
reaction, and hydraulic cavitation, have been developed to produce nanobubbles [33]. The 
184 
hydraulic cavitation method is widely recognized as an effective approach, capable of rapidly 
185 
producing numerous nanobubbles (>107 bubbles·mL−1) within minutes [14,34], which was 
186 
used in this study. After operating the system for 15 mins, the system achieved concentrations 
187 
of N2 (Fig. 1a) and CO2 (Fig. 1b) nanobubbles at 1.43 × 108 and 5.46 × 107 bubbles·mL−1, 
188 
respectively. Although the generator is different, the obtained nanobubble concentrations 
189 
(107-108 bubbles·mL−1) were consistent with those reported in other studies using hydraulic 
190 
cavitation-based approaches [35]. As the operation time increased from 5 to 15 min, the 
191 
generated H2 nanobubble concentrations remained at the level of 107 bubbles·mL−1, which 
192 
may be due to the low viscosity, small molecular size, and high solubility of H2, which makes 
193 
H2 nanobubbles less stable. Therefore, a H2 nanobubble solution containing a concentration 
194 
of 4.04 × 107 bubbles·mL−1 (Fig. 1c) was used in this mechanistic exploration study. Additionally, 
195 
more than 99% of the nanobubbles in the three solutions exhibited a size below 400 nm. 
196 
Moreover, in addition to the formation of bubbles at the nanoscale, the process of generating 
197 
nanobubbles could lead to the (super) saturation of gas in the liquid solution as the 
198 
nanobubble slowly released the gas [36]. Therefore, the pH of the N2, CO2, and H2 nanobubble 
199 
solutions were 8.66 ± 0.23, 4.35 ± 0.08 and 5.50 ± 0.12, respectively.

3.4. Implementation potential and research needs 
448 


[{"type": "pdf_block", "pdf_page": 18, "pdf_page_label": "", "block": 13, "bbox_points": [36.36000061035156, 249.04798889160156, 527.1131591796875, 444.50396728515625]}]
The application of nanobubble technology in AD is an area of active research, but scientific 
449 
and technical challenges still need to be addressed. Nanobubbles featured a negatively 
450 
charged zeta potential, which led to increased charge repulsion between particles, preventing 
451 
particle agglomeration [57]. Additionally, nanobubbles exhibited a longer spin–spin relaxation 
452 
time (T2) than normal water molecules, which represents a higher water molecular 
453 
mobility[20]. This increased mobility can promote full contact between microorganisms and 
454 
organic matter, thereby facilitating nutrients transportation. Higher water mobility may 
455 
improve the fluidity of digested solutions and enhance mass transfer efficiency. In this study, 
456 
the effect of nanobubble on hydrogen production from rice straw hydrolysis was better than 
457 
that on methane production, indicating that nanobubbles altered the anaerobic digestion 
458 
pathway of rice straw, shifting it toward alcohol or other substances. 
459 


[{"type": "pdf_block", "pdf_page": 18, "pdf_page_label": "", "block": 14, "bbox_points": [36.36000061035156, 452.4479675292969, 527.1119995117188, 612.0239868164062]}]
In terms of engineering applications, the use of reflux biogas slurry instead of water as the 
460 
carrier of nanobubbles will enhance the practicality of the process. This study shows that 
461 
nanobubble has a more obvious promotion effect on the hydrolytic acidification stage 
462 
compared with the methanogenesis stage. Therefore, nanobubbles should be used in the 
463 
hydrolytic acidification tank of two-phase anaerobic fermentation systems. In addition, 
464 
nanobubble technology can significantly promote H2 production during anaerobic 
465 
fermentation. In the future, this technology can be used to improve the dark fermentation 
466 
process. Certainly, further research can investigate the feasibility and efficiency of 
467 
implementing this strategy in a full-scale, long-term anaerobic digestion system. 
468
