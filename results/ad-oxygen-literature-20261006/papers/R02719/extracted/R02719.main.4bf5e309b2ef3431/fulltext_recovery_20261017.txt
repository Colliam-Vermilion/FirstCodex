ABSTRACT 
19 
 
20 
This study evaluated the performance of a micro-aerobic hydrolysis of mixed sludge and its 
21 
influence as a pretreatment of this waste for its subsequent anaerobic digestion. Three 
22 
experimental series were carried out to evaluate the optimum micro aeration levels in the 
23 
range from 0.1 to 0.5 vvm and operation times within the range 24-60 h. The maximum

29 
This methane yield value increased 114% with respect to that obtained with the non-aerated 
30

INTRODUCTION 
41 
 
42 
The anaerobic process, commonly called anaerobic digestion,  is the most used 
43 
treatment technology for organic matter removal from primary and waste activated sludge  
44 
(WAS).[1-3] The benefits associated with anaerobic technology include mass reduction, odor 
45 
removal,[4-5]  pathogen reduction, and more significantly, energy recovery in the form of 
46 
methane.[6]  However, it should be emphasized that sludge contains 10 times the energy 
47 
required to treat it.  It has been proven to be technically feasible to recover energy from the 
48 


[PDF page 3]
3 
 
sludge, which can be directly used in wastewater treatment or be sold to the network, 
49 
reducing the facility´s dependency on conventional electricity and helping the stressed 
50 
public budgets.  Nevertheless, the anaerobic digestion of sewage sludge has several 
51 
disadvantages such as relatively low methane production, 30 - 50 % biodegradability and 
52 
the presence of some inhibitory compounds that make necessary the use of high retention 
53 
times in the digesters with high mixing costs.[7-9] These limiting factors are generally 
54 
associated with the hydrolysis stage.[10-11] During hydrolysis, cell walls are ruptured and 
55 
extracelular polymeric substances (EPS) are degraded resulting in the release of readily 
56 
available organic material for the acidogenic microorganisms. This mechanism is 
57 
particularly important in mixed sludge digestion, since the major constituent of its organic 
58 
fraction are cells, being a relatively unfavorable substrate for microbial degradation.  
59 
In order to avoid or reduce the existence of the hydrolysis process in the sludge anaerobic 
60 
digestion several methods have been studied, alone or in combination such as thermal[12-14]  
61 
, physical-chemical[15-16] and mechanical.[17-18] Some biological methods have been also 
62 
investigated.[19-20] 
63 
Among biological, the use of micro aeration as a treatment prior to anaerobic digestion has 
64 
received less attention, even though it has been reported that the limited oxygen supply 
65 
caused an increase of the enzymatic hydrolysis rates for the case of complex organic matter 
66 
in batch tests.[21] Results from some micro aerobic research carried out with municipal solid 
67 
wastes and other wastes suggest that hydrolysis, and therefore, the COD solubilization 
68 
might be improved by micro aeration.[22-24] However, the presence of oxygen in the 
69 
anaerobic digestion can also produce a problem regarding to the use and degradation of 
70 
organic matter. In presence of oxygen, there is a competition by the substrate between 
71 
facultative and strict anaerobes[25], which could reduce the organic matter available for 
72 


[PDF page 4]
4 
 
methanogenic archaea, because part of the substrate is used for the production of CO2 and 
73 
biomass by facultative microorganisms. Therefore, the management of micro-aeration 
74 
should be carefully carry out in order to improve the hydrolytic rate with a minimum 
75 
consume of organic matter by facultative microorganisms with CO2 and biomass 
76 
production. 
77 
Regarding the effect of micro-aeration on sewage sludge anaerobic digestion, few studies 
78 
have been reported. Jenicek et al.[26], using a continuous bioreactor and pumping air 
79 
continuously into the sludge recirculation stream, showed that VSS/TSS ratio of the 
80 
digested sludge, soluble COD concentration, ammonia nitrogen and phosphate 
81 
concentration decreased in all experiments with micro aerobic conditions. Jenicek et al.[27]  
82 
working with a continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) under continuous micro aeration, 
83 
indicated that efficient H2S removal, higher specific methane production, lower methane 
84 
concentration in biogas due to dilution by nitrogen remained from the air dosed were 
85 
achieved in the anaerobic digestion of the pre-aerated sludge, while better sludge liquor 
86 
quality and lower foaming potential and foam stability were observed in the pre-treated 
87 
sludge after using micro aeration. Montalvo et al.[28] determined that applying 0.3 vvm, and 
88 
at temperature of 35 C were the best operational conditions increasing hydrolysis rate of 
89 
mixed sludge. However they did not find the optimum amount of air for hydrolysis nor 
90 
tested the effect of aerobic hydrolysis to different aeration rates on anaerobic digestion 
91 
sludge 
92 
Given this background, the behavior and performance of the micro aerobic hydrolysis of 
93 
mixed sewage sludge looking for the optimum level of aeration were evaluated in the 
94 
present research work with the aim of achieving maximum efficiency of hydrolysis. The 
95 
effect of the micro-aeration level of the mixed sludge on the methane yield was also 
96 


[PDF page 5]
5 
 
evaluated in biochemical methane potential tests carried out at 35 ºC in batch mode, 
97 
comparing the results obtained with the pre-aerated sludge with those achieved for control 
98 
sludge without pre-aeration.

MATERIALS AND METHODS 
101 
 
102 
Three experimental runs were carried out at laboratory-scale. A summary of the operating 
103 
conditions assayed in each experimental run is shown in Table 1. 
104 
The substrate used in all the assays was a mixed sludge (mixture of waste activated sludge 
105 
and primary sludge). This mixed sludge was obtained from the urban wastewater treatment 
106 
plant (UWTP) called “La Farfana”, which is located in Santiago de Chile city. Each studied 
107 
experimental run was carried out by duplicate.  
108 
The experimental run I was performed with one sample of mixed sludge while the 
109 
experimental series II and III were carried out with four samples of mixed sludge taken 
110 
during different times along the day with the aim of obtaining more representative results. 
111 
In the experimental run I, batch reactors of 5 L of total volume and 4 L of working volume 
112 
were used for the micro aeration process. After micro aerobic pretreatment, 200 mL of 
113 
pretreated sludge is passed into batch anaerobic digesters of 280 mL of total volume. After 
114 
that, 50 mL of anaerobic inoculum is added to each anaerobic digester. The anaerobic 
115 
inoculum came from the anaerobic plants that digest waste activated sludge at “La Farfana” 
116 
UWTP. This anaerobic inoculum had a VSS concentration of 10 g/L and a specific 
117 
methanogenic activity of 0.32 g COD-CH4/(g VSS·d). The anaerobic digesters were sealed 
118 
and headspace flushed with N2 at the beginning of the test. The produced biogas was 
119 
measured by liquid displacement after going through a 3% (w/w) NaOH solution to capture 
120 


[PDF page 6]
6 
 
the produced CO2; the remaining gas was assumed to be only methane. The anaerobic tests 
121 
were run for a period of c.a. 20-25 days until the accumulated gas production remained 
122 
essentially unchanged, i.e. on the last day production was lower than 3% of the 
123 
accumulated methane produced. The main objective of this experimental run was to obtain 
124 
the best aeration level within the range from 0.1 to 0.5 vvm (volume of air/(volume of 
125 
reactor · minute)) taken into account as criterion the methane production achieved in the 
126 
batch anaerobic digestion experiment of the pre-aerated sludge. The experiment 
127 
corresponding to an aeration level of 0.3 vvm was extended until 60 h with the aim of 
128 
assessing the evolution of proteins and total sugars with time.     
129 
The experimental run II had two main objectives: firstly, to obtain the optimum aeration 
130 
level and, secondly, to assess the influence of aeration time on the hydrolysis process. After 
131 
this run, no batch anaerobic digestion experiments of the pre-aerated sludge were made. 
132 
Finally in the experimental run III, the performance of the micro-aerated hydrolysis was 
133 
evaluated from the evolution of different parameters such as soluble proteins and sugars 
134 
with time and its influence on the methane production of the pre-aerated sludge. In this run 
135 
II, it was used the best aeration level obtained in the second experimental run (0.35 vvm). 
136 
The operating temperature selected for the micro aerobic hydrolysis experiments was 35 ºC 
137 
according to previous results reported in the literature.[28] This temperature was also used 
138 
for batch anaerobic digestion experiments of the pretreated sludge because it coincides with 
139 
the optimum temperature reported for achieving maximum methane production within the 
140 
mesophilic interval.[7]        
141 
All chemical analysis were determined according to American Public Health Association 
142 
Standard Methods.[29] Specifically, chemical oxygen demand (COD) and volatile suspended 
143 
solids were analysed according to the closed digestion and colorimetric 5220D method and 
144 


[PDF page 7]
7 
 
2540B method, respectively of the Standard Methods (APHA, 2012).[29] pH was determined 
145 
using a pH-meter model Crison 20 Basic. 
146 
Soluble proteins and total sugars were determined using the ASTM D5712 and ASTM 
147 
D6406 standard methods, respectively.  
148 
For the statistic processing and analysis of the data, the software Minitab 8 was utilized. An 
149 
ANOVA analysis was done and a comparison of confidence intervals for mean values was 
150 
made with a confidence of 95%.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION   
153 
 
154 
No significant differences in the behavior of duplicate reactors were observed in all assays. 
155 
Therefore, only the results of one of the duplicate reactors will be shown.   
156 
 
157 
Assay I 
158 
 
159 
Figure 1 shows the evolution of the methane production (measured as mL methane/g of 
160 
volatile suspended solids (VSS) added), which will allow to assess the variation of the 
161 
accumulated methane with time per each gram of VSS added at the beginning of the 
162 
anaerobic digestion process. 
163 
As can be seen for an aeration level of 0.1 vvm only a small increase in methane production 
164 
was observed compared to the control (not subjected to aeration). An ANOVA analysis of 
165 
the data revealed that this difference in production of methane was not statistically 
166 
significant with a confidence level of 95%. In this case, the following values of statistical 
167 
parameters were obtained: F=0.141; p=0.709; Fcritical=4.130 (Fcritical value was higher than 
168 


[PDF page 8]
8 
 
F). By contrast, for an aeration level of 0.3 vvm an increase of 114% in the methane 
169 
production of the pre-aerated sample was achieved compared to the control without 
170 
aeration. In this case, the difference was statistically significant as was shown in the 
171 
statistical analysis (F = 6.789; p = 0.013; Fcritical = 4.149). Finally, for the experimental run 
172 
of 0.5 vvm, no significant differences between the methane production of the pre-aerated 
173 
sludge and the control were detected (F=0.044; p=0.834; Fcritical=4.130), which 
174 
demonstrates that a high aeration may not be beneficial, and, even, harmful for the 
175 
anaerobic digestion process.     
176 
Therefore, according to these results an aeration level of 0.3 vvm contributes to better 
177 
performance of methanogenic microorganisms confirmed by the methane production 
178 
obtained in this case. When the aeration level increased up to 0.5 vvm a decrease in 
179 
methane production was observed as consequence of the higher concentration of dissolved 
180 
oxygen, which inhibited the metabolism and activity of methanogens. Gerritse and 
181 
Gottschal[30] demonstrated that an aeration level of 0.1 vvm did not increase the methane 
182 
formation compared to a control sample. However, a short time of exposure to oxygen did 
183 
not reduce the methanogenic activity and under these conditions the methanogens can 
184 
survive more time than previously expected.[31]  For instance, oxygen concentration levels 
185 
in the range of 4.9-6.4 mg/L are required to inhibit 50% of the methanogenic activity.[32]  
186 
All these results clearly indicated that an adequate optimization of oxygen supply to 
187 
aeration is required in all cases to avoid the inhibition of methanogenic activity.   
188 
As was shown in Figure 1, the maximum methane yield (35 mL CH4/g VSS added) was 
189 
obtained for an aeration level of 0.3 vvm. This value represents an increase of 114% in 
190 
methane production compared to that obtained with the non-aerated sludge. This increase in 
191 
the methanogenic activity is attributed to an improvement in the growth of facultative 
192 


[PDF page 9]
9 
 
anaerobic microorganisms, which can keep a low redox potential, providing the best 
193 
conditions for the growth of strict anaerobes.[23]   
194 
Table 2 shows the effect of aeration on the final pH of the reactors. As can be seen, a pH 
195 
increase of 7.1% and 3.2% was achieved in the reactors with aeration and non-aeration, 
196 
respectively. This pH increase can be due to two main factors: firstly, the hydrolysis of 
197 
proteins produces the liberation of amino groups (-NH2), which are converted into 
198 
ammonia (NH3), which when is dissolved generates ammonium (NH4
+) and hydroxyl ions 
199 
(OH-), causing a pH increase.[33]   Second, when micro-aeration is applied, a CO2 stripping 
200 
takes place by the air supplied to the process.  
201 
Figures 2 shows the evolution of the soluble proteins (measured as equivalent concentration 
202 
of bovine serum albumin, BSA) during the micro-aeration process at 0.3 vvm of the three 
203 
samples of sludge. 
204 
As can be seen in Figure 2, an increase in the soluble proteins of 185% was observed after 
205 
48 h of aeration (at 0.3 vvm), while that an increase of only 7.8% was detected in the non-
206 
aerated reactor. The evolution of the soluble proteins with time had a behavior of bell-
207 
shaped, achieving a maximum value at 48 h of aeration. This behavior can be explained 
208 
taking into consideration that a part of the mixed sludge is composed by waste activated 
209 
sludge, which consisted of flocs formed by clusters of cells and organic matter within a 
210 
viscous material. For this reason, there was an increase in soluble proteins until 48 h, which 
211 
were released and at the same time the insoluble proteins retained in the flocs could also be 
212 
solubilized.  This process is called de-flocculation, and is caused, among other factors, by a 
213 
decrease in the aeration of the activated sludge process, reducing the amount of dissolved 
214 
oxygen, which coincides with the operating parameters in the present work, where low air 
215 


[PDF page 10]
10 
 
flows were injected, causing the low amount of dissolved oxygen in the reactors, its 
216 
concentration was found to be zero in all measurements carried out. 
217 
Another possible explanation of the increase in the protein concentration during the first 48 
218 
h of aeration is the solubilization of insoluble proteins found in the organic matter of the 
219 
sludge by proteases enzymes. 
220 
Finally, once the maximum protein concentration was achieved at 48 h, a decrease in the 
221 
protein content was observed due to its degradation by non-hydrolytic bacteria. 
222 
Figure 3 shows evolution of total sugars (measured as equivalent concentration of glucose).  
223 
As it is known hydrolysis is the rate-limiting step for the carbohydrates conversion.[34]  
224 
Therefore, the total sugar concentration also increased in a similar manner during the first 
225 
48 h of aeration, obtaining an increase of 192% with respect of its initial value (Figure 3). 
226 
This rise can be mainly attributed to the hydrolysis of polysaccharides to monosaccharides 
227 
when micro-aeration is applied, which is extremely beneficial for anaerobic digestion. A 
228 
maximum value in the sugar concentration was achieved at 48 h of aeration, after which a 
229 
soft decrease was detected. This may be attributed to a certain conversion of 
230 
polysaccharides after this time and the beginning of the transformation of the 
231 
monosaccharides. By contrast, the increase in the total sugars in the non-aerated reactor 
232 
was only of 3%. 
233 
Table 3 summarizes the values of the concentration of total ammonia (TAMON), total 
234 
COD (CODT), soluble COD (CODS) and VSS (average of the three samples of mixed 
235 
sludge) after micro-aeration (0.3 vvm) during 48 h and after non-aeration conditions.      
236 
The results shown in Table 3 indicate that the micro aeration process of mixed sludge 
237 
influence the significant increase of ammonia nitrogen, which is due to the hydrolysis of 
238 
proteins and their further conversion to amino-acids and ammonia nitrogen. This agrees 
239 


[PDF page 11]
11 
 
with the increase of pH from 5.8 to 6.0-6.2, which results beneficial for the anaerobic 
240 
digestion process of the pre-aerated sludge. Despite the increase in ammonia concentration 
241 
up to values of 920 mg/L, this value cannot be considered as toxic o inhibitory for 
242 
anaerobic process, whose values range from 1500-3000 mg/L[35-36] The CODS also 
243 
increased both in the non-aerated as in the micro-aerated reactors. However, in the reactors 
244 
with micro-aeration, the CODS increased 150% with respect of its initial value, while in the 
245 
non-aerated ones this increase was only of 27%. These values showed that micro-aeration 
246 
promotes the hydrolysis of the complex organic matter and its conversion to soluble matter 
247 
with a considerable rise of the CODS as a result of the enzymatic reaction.[37] 
248 
On the other hand, the CODT experimented a relatively small decrease in both cases 
249 
studied, being this decrease quite less for the non-aerated reactor. This implies that the 
250 
complex organic matter can be transformed into more simple matter but never to develop a 
251 
removal process of organic matter aerobically, which would imply a much higher energy 
252 
expenditure.   
253 
Finally, the VSS concentration also decreased in all reactors, although this decrease was 
254 
quite higher in the micro-aerated reactors (40%) compared to that achieved in the control 
255 
reactors (11%). This again shows the micro-aeration promote the hydrolysis of the complex 
256 
organic matter, and this matter is precisely the substrate of enzymatic reaction.[38]

Assay II

Figure 4 shows the evolution of the CODS with time for each one of the four samples of 
261 
mixed sludge (A, B, C and D) subjected to micro-aerobic hydrolysis using aeration levels 
262 
of 0.3, 0.35, 0.4, 0.45 and 0.5 vvm. As can be seen, an increase in CODS with the aeration 
263 


[PDF page 12]
12 
 
time was observed in most cases. Specifically, increases in CODS of between 100% and 
264 
383% were observed for the three experimental runs carried out (Figures 8 B, C and D) 
265 
when the aeration intensity increased from 0.3 to 0.5 vvm. Therefore, when aeration was 
266 
increased between the mentioned values, the CODS was at least doubled. For the best 
267 
aeration level tested (0.35 vvm) an increase in the solubility from 8% to 23% was obtained 
268 
when the aeration time increased from 48 to 60 h. 
269 
The variation of the VSS with time for the four experimental runs carried are with different 
270 
aeration intensities is shown in Figure 5. A decrease in the VSS content was observed for 
271 
all the cases studied. VSS reduction percentages in the range from 11.5% to 23.6% were 
272 
achieved after 60 h of aeration. This reduction represents the decomposition of the complex 
273 
organic matter that is transformed in the substrate of the hydrolytic reactions. The highest 
274 
VSS removal was reached with an aeration level of 0.35 vvm. Therefore, the use of a 
275 
controlled aeration with a specific aeration level allow eliminating certain non-
276 
biodegradable compounds or compounds more difficult to biodegrade using only anaerobic 
277 
digestion.[39]  VSS reductions of between 2-5% were only reached between the 48 and 60 h 
278 
of aeration for the experiments carried out with an aeration level of 0.35 vvm. 
279 
Finally, the VSS removal percentages obtained at an aeration level of 0.35 vvm (23.6%) 
280 
were higher than others reported in the literature using an increase of temperature between 
281 
40 and 50ºC (20% VSS reduction) instead of a micro-aeration step.[40]

Figure 7 illustrates the variation of the VSS with digestion time for the anaerobic digestion

of micro-aerated sludge and control. As can be seen a clear decrease in the VSS content

296

Figure 8 shows the variation of methane production (measured as accumulated CH4 in 
299 
mL/g VSS added) as a function of the digestion time, which allows evaluating the volume 
300 
of methane produced per each gram of VSS added at the beginning of the anaerobic 
301 
digestion process. According to the data shown in this Figure, a significant increase (110%) 
302 
in the production of methane was obtained when applying to the mixed sludge aerobic 
303 
hydrolysis compared to the digestion process of non-aerated sludge. These results are 
304 
consistent with the evolution of the COD and VSS presented previously. Lower increments 
305 
in the methane yield (21%) were previously reported during the anaerobic co-digestion of 
306 
brown water and food waste (during 45 day operation time) previously subjected to an 
307 
micro-aerobic pretreatment with an aeration intensity of 0.0375 L O2/Lreactor/day.[41]

CONCLUSIONS 
310 
 
311 


[PDF page 14]
14 
 
This study demonstrated that biological hydrolysis by micro aeration of mixed sludge from 
312 
urban wastewater treatment plants is an effective pretreatment of this waste for its 
313 
subsequent anaerobic digestion. The most efficient aeration level to increase significantly 
314 
the methane yield of the micro aerated sludge compared to a control without pre-aeration 
315 
was 0.35 vvm. Although higher COD increases and VSS decreases were found at 60 h of 
316 
micro aeration, the above parameters did not vary significantly with respect to the values 
317 
found at 48 h. Therefore, it would be necessary carefully evaluate the need to extend the 
318 
aeration for more than 48 h since the process improvement was not significantly increased 
319 
after 2 days of aeration. The application of a hydrolysis by micro aeration also allowed 
320 
stimulating the production of exoenzymes that carried out the degradation of slowly 
321 
biodegradable compounds which were otherwise resistant to degradation under completely 
322 
anaerobic conditions.
