Adsorption of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) on Activated Carbon

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 11, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
Due to its cost-effectiveness, adsorption on activated carbon (AC) generated from organic components has recently been deemed the most effective approach for removing pollutants from wastewater. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) has been removed from gas mixtures and liquids using AC [66]. Normally, various parameters influence the adsorption process, including porosity, surface area, functional groups, activation mechanism, and adsorbate situations [60]. It is critical to investigate the organization of precursor materials and the suitable agent for the activation process to functionalize the adsorbent’s surface chemistry to improve the adsorption capacity [7]. Among different impregnating agents that have been in use to increase the adsorption capacity of AC in H2S removal include impregnating with iodine [85, 134], metal hydroxides, and oxides [21, 43, 75, 121], carbonates [37, 142, 236] and nitrogen groups using urea or melamine [102, 183]. Several investigations have revealed that chemically AC is more effective than physically AC and has a good adsorption performance. For example, the results indicated that the chemical activation of palm shells using sulfuric acid (H2SO4) or potassium hydroxide (KOH) activation had better performance than the thermal activation by carbondioxide (CO2). This happens due to CO2 and H2S competing for adsorption sites on the surface of activated carbon. As a result, elevated CO2 levels may lower activated carbon's ability to effectively absorb H2S [120]. The palm shell AC eliminates H2S through chemisorption, physisorption, and/or H2S oxidation [85]. However, AC technology has often been confined to pore saturation, necessitating constant replacement and regeneration. Numerous methods and techniques have been used to improve AC's performance during the adsorption process. Regulated steam pretreatment, for example, is used to enhance the features of (AC), with the steam-treated AC electrode exhibiting improved electrochemical attributes. According to Zhen-YuLi, when the steam pretreatment was employed at 700 °C with the gasification process, the surface properties of AC improved in a high specific capacitance of 187.2 F/g (Farad/gram) was recorded compared with bare AC electrode exhibited low capacitance of 155.2 F/g. Therefore,  these conditions were set to be optimal for enriching the higher surface area and porosity of AC [126]. After 1000 cycles, the steam-treated AC electrode demonstrated outstanding stability, with an initial capacity of 92%. Other chemical approaches for improving activated carbon surface are more expensive in some ways due to the need for changes and surface modifications of the AC. However, it has been discovered that by impregnating AC with composite materials made by balancing the proportion of transition metal elements and metal oxides, the surface chemistry of AC can be improved [85]. For example, adding some transition metals to the surface chemistry of carbonaceous adsorbents can improve sulfide removal [183]. This research confirmed that various hydroxide composites or metal oxides containing graphite oxide (GO) could impact the H2S adsorption process.

Stripping method

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 13, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
Another sulfide removal method is stripping, a physical system in which air and wastewater flow in opposite directions. Mass transfer underlies how stripping functions. The goal is to improve the two components' environment. One component must cross a gas-liquid interface to reach the vapour phase [116]. Other methods for capturing H2S before release to the atmosphere include bio-trickling filtration, scrubbing, absorption, and common physical solvents such as alkanol-amines [163]. Also, the findings of Glória and her colleagues  [78] showed that hydrogen sulfide removal effectiveness was between 40 and 60 % using an air stripping technique. However, this process is not cost-effective because it necessitates the construction of a stripping tower, which might be costly.

Wet scrubbing

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 15, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) may be removed from biogas using water as an absorbent since it is more soluble in water than methane. In addition, hydrogen sulfide may be physically removed from the water because it exists as a gas when air is injected, letting H2S escape. The H2S released from biogas can then be controlled using packed-bed wet scrubbers coated with titanium (IV) oxide (TiO2) nanoparticles [50].

2.2 Anaerobic (anoxic) biological technology

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 19, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
The term "anoxic" describes the environment rather than individual bacteria or their functions. The presence or absence of nitrogen distinguishes anoxic conditions and anaerobic conditions in wastewater treatment. Nitrates or nitrites may be present in anoxic environments, despite the lack of molecular oxygen [164]. The experiment showed that at an EBRT of 300 s, expanded schist efficiently removed H2S concentration up to 1100 ppmv [93].

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 20, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
Meanwhile, Mahmood and other co-authors [132] described that the removal efficiencies of nitrite and H2S in the anoxic sulfide reactor were attained at 75% and 99%, respectively. This was achieved in their work while testing the potential of an anoxic sulfide oxidizing (ASO) reactor for simultaneous sulfide and nitrite removal from wastewater. The results were reported over 15 days, during which time the sulfide loading rate was used as a critical metric for evaluating the potential of a bioreactor. In general, it was determined that the loading rate, substrate concentration, and  HRT all play a significant role in evaluating the reactor's performance and nitrite and H2S removal efficiencies [47]. Sulfide removal efficiencies were higher than 88.97%, as depicted by the sulfide loading rate ranging between 0.02 and 0.96 kg/ (m3 day). However, there was a progressive decline in sulfide removal percentage over the final 15 days of operation as the effluent sulfide concentration was increased. On the other hand, the effect of hydraulic retention time revealed that at 0.10 day HRT, the highest sulfide and nitrite removal rates were 13.82 and 16.31 kg/(m3 day), respectively. Finally, when HRT was reduced at a fixed amount of substrate, the reactor performance was better than when the amount of substrate was elevated at a fixed HRT.

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 21, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
In anoxic bioreactors, the sulfide to nitrate (S/N) molar ratio is also a key determinant of biogas desulfurization efficacy [13, 111]. According to several studies, when the amount of nitrate () is inadequate, H2S is solely converted into elemental sulfur.

[{"type": "word_paragraph", "paragraph": 22, "section_heading": null, "style": "Normal"}]
The optimized C/N ratio in the anoxic bioreactors plays a key role in improving the AD process to increase methane content and decrease hydrogen sulfide formation. However, the initial C/N ratio is affected by several feedstocks with an imbalanced amount of carbon to nitrogen. In fact, the C/N ratio can be adjusted by co-digesting different feedstocks with higher levels of carbon or nitrogen sources in order to maximize the desired C/N ratio for an enhanced desulfurization process and overall anoxic bioreactor efficiency [46].
