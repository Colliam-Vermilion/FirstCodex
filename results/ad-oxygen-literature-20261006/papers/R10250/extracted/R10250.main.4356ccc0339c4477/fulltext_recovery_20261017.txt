Exploring nanobubble technology for enhanced anaerobic digestion of

A B S T R A C T
Nanobubble technology was used to enhance anaerobic digestion (AD) of thermal-hydrolysis pre-treated sewage
sludge for bioenergy recovery. The prepared air, CO2, and H2 nanobubble solutions, with concentrations of
9.88–10.2 × 107 bubbles/mL, remained stable for at least 7 days. After adding them into AD reactors, signifi-
cantly higher CH4 production (37.1 %) was observed for the CO2 nanobubble treatment, followed by air (25.6 %)
and H2 (14.5 %) nanobubble treatments, compared to the control group. CO2 nanobubble treatment performed
the best in improving acidogenesis/acetogenesis, resulting in significantly higher volatile fatty acid generation
during the initial 3–4 days. A comparison of reactors supersaturated and non-saturated with oxygen has
demonstrated most of the biogas uplift observed to result from the nanobubbles rather than from initial oxygen
soluble levels, demonstrating the crucial role of nanobubbles in upgrading AD. This study demonstrates, for the
first time, that nanobubbles can provide additional benefits when combined with stablished sludge pre-treatment
technologies.
1. Introduction
Anaerobic digestion (AD) is widely used for treating waste biomass
while recovering valuable products like biogas and organic fertiliser
(Kizito et al., 2017; Rocamora et al., 2020). In the water sector, the
generation of renewable energy from AD-sourced biogas facilitates
achieving energy self-sustained wastewater treatment plants and aligns
with the sector-wide Net-Zero commitment (Farag`o et al., 2022).
Consequently, numerous efforts have focused on optimising AD pro-
cesses to achieve better CH4 yields (Li et al., 2019). Among the strategies
investigated, the introduction of a small amount of air or O2 into the
system, a process known as microaeration, has been reported to be as a
cost-effective strategy for promoting the growth of facultative hydrolytic
and acidogenic microorganisms by providing a favourable environment
that enhances their metabolic activities and enzyme production,
consequently enhancing CH4 yield (Chen et al., 2020). Al-mashhadani
et al. (2016) further demonstrated that smaller bubbles, i.e. N2 micro-
bubbles (Ø in μm), could reduce the Gibbs free energy and benefit the
shifting of the thermodynamic equilibrium for increased CH4 produc-
tion. The existence of even smaller bubbles (Ø < 1 μm), referred to as
nanobubbles or ultra-fine bubbles, was first experimentally confirmed
through atomic force microscopy imaging in 2000 (Lyu et al., 2019).
Attributed to their unique properties, including high gas transfer effi-
ciency, remarkable longevity, and the ability to generate reactive oxy-
gen species (ROS) (Atkinson et al., 2019), nanobubble technology has
been attempted to improve AD performance since 2019.
The addition of pre-prepared nanobubble solutions to ADs treating
lignocellulosic feedstocks (lignin and cellulose) has been reported to
increase CH4 yield by 7.7 to 29.9 % (Ho et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2020c)
(Table 1). Similar enhancements were found in ADs processing waste
activated sludge (11–29.1 %), livestock waste (−5.2–39.3 %), food
waste (14–43 %), lignocellulosic crop waste (−1.23–46.8 %), and co-

Despite promising results, several knowledge gaps remain in the
wider application and mechanistic understanding of nanobubble tech-
nology enhanced AD. At large sewage treatment plants, thermal-
hydrolysis pretreatment has become a common step to breakdown
organic compounds and improve the biodegradability of sludge prior to
sludge digestion (Higgins et al., 2011). There is still a lack of evidence
regarding the adaptability and efficiency of nanobubble technology to
existing AD plants equipped with such sludge pre-treatment. Moreover,
nanobubble solutions can result in a gas supersaturated conditions (Fan
et al., 2021). Considering the known enhancement of AD performance
through microaeration, wherein a small amount of air or O2 is intro-
duced, it is unknown how the supersaturation of the system contributes
to CH4 yield enhancements as opposed to the action of the nanobubbles
themselves.
This study aims to evaluate and advance the application of nano-
bubble technology in AD, particularly in a deployable sludge digestion
scenario. Prepared solutions of air, H2, and CO2 nanobubbles were
monitored to assess their stability in terms of bubble size distribution
and concentrations. The impacts of these three types of nanobubbles on
biogas and CH4 generation in ADs preceded by thermal hydrolysis pre-
treatment were compared. Moreover, the effects of supersaturated and
non-supersaturated nanobubble solutions were compared to distinguish
the contributions of dissolved gas and nanobubbles. The first-order ki-
netic model and the modified Gompertz model were used to assess the
dynamics on CH4 production in different treatment groups and explore
the potential mechanisms. Additionally, the dynamics of volatile fatty
acids in all reactors were determined to support the understanding of the
enhancement mechanisms. With the results, this study could facilitate
the deployment of novel nanobubble technology in existing ADs,
contributing towards sustainable sewage sludge treatment and renew-
able energy generation.
2. Materials and methods
2.1. Nanobubble generation and characterisation
Various methods, including hydrodynamic cavitation, ultrasonic
fragmentation, electrolysis, and porous membranes, have been devel-
oped to generate nanobubbles. Among these, the hydrodynamic method
is widely recognised as an effective approach, capable of rapidly
producing a large quantity of nanobubbles (>107 bubbles/mL) within
relatively short time. In this study, three gases (air, H2 and CO2) were
selected to produce targeted nanobubble solutions using a commercially
available hydrodynamic cavitation based nanobubble generator (Nikuni
KTM20N, Aeration & Mixing Ltd., UK) connected via hoses to a cylin-
drical tank filled with 50 L of deionised water (Fig. 1a). The nanobubble
generator operated with an inlet pressure of −0.03 MPa and an outlet
pressure of 0.4 MPa. Air, H2, and CO2 gases (99.99 %, BOC Gases,
Guildford, UK) were separately introduced into the pump at a gas flow
rate of 1.5 L/min and blended with the deionised water. To determine
the optimal operational duration, triplicate 50 mL samples were taken
using pipette at 5, 10, 15 and 20 mins after starting the device. The
samples were stored in clean 50 mL centrifuge tubes for analysis of
nanobubble size distribution and concentration by a nanoparticle
tracking instrument (NanoSight LM20, Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK).
Nanoparticle tracking and analysis technology is a non-contact mea-
surement method based on optical principle, which irradiates the
nanoparticles by laser beam to make the particles scatter light, the
scattered light is converted into electric signal by photoelectric con-
verter, and then the information such as the size, concentration and
motion state of the particles is analysed by signal processing system. The
particle gradation (D10, D50, D90) were recorded and calculated the
span value to evaluate the range of NB size. Tests for nanobubble sta-
bility were also conducted on aliquots collected after 15-min of opera-
tion after 7 days of storage in a sample bottle at ambient temperature. To
prepare the non-supersaturated air bubble solutions, the freshly pre-
pared air-nanobubble solution was left in the tank (Fig. 1a) for 72 h,
until the dissolved oxygen (DO) level (HQ30d, Hach Co., USA) dropped
to the theoretical oxygen solubility at room temperature (~9 mg/L).
2.2. Batch anaerobic digestion (AD) experiments
2.2.1. Feedstock sludge and inoculum
Municipal sewage sludge and digestate used as feedstock and inoc-
ulum for the AD trials were collected from a full-scale wastewater
treatment plant treating a population equivalent of 2.57 million. The
feedstock sludge comprised a blend of primary sludge and waste acti-
vated sludge from the site’s activated sludge process. The blended
sludge was subjected to thermal hydrolysis pre-treatment (THP) at 6–7
bars pressure and 160–165 ◦C. After the pre-treatment, the total solids
(TS) and volatile solids (VS) of the substrate were 102.8 and 79.8 g/L,
respectively, determined by standard methods (APHA, 1998). The
inoculum was collected from one of the site’s anaerobic digesters, with
concentrations of TS and VS at 58.1 and 36.4 g/L, respectively. Both
feedstock sludge and inoculum were stored in 5 L sealed sample box in
the wastewater treatment plant. The materials were transported to the

2.2.2. Biochemical methane potential (BMP) test
BMP tests were conducted in 1-L batch stirred reactors (CJC Labs
Ltd., UK) immersed in a water bath to maintain a mesophilic tempera-
ture at 38 ◦C (Fig. 1b). Each reactor was fed with inoculum (395 mL) and
feedstock sludge (90 mL), with an inoculum to substrate VS ratio of 2.
For the control group, an additional 30 mL of deionised water was added
to the reactor, while the same amount of nanobubble solution was added
to the test reactors. Four types of nanobubble solution, i.e. air (super-
saturated), air (non-supersaturated), H2, and CO2, were tested. The
batch AD experiment lasted 30 days and the biogas production was
continuously monitored using tipping bucket gas counters (CJC-048,
CJC Labs Ltd., UK). The gas tightness of the reactor was tested with a
nitrogen charge prior to start-up of each reactor. CH4 concentration in
the biogas was determined via Gas Chromatography (Valco Instruments
Company Inc., USA) every two days. A blank control group with
equivalent volume of inoculum and deionised water was operated.
Biogas and CH4 yields (mL/g VSadded) were calculated from their accu-
mulated production in mL subtracted by the accumulated biogas pro-
duction from the blank and normalising to the mass of VS from the
sewage sludge fed to the reactor.
Besides experimental CH4 production (EMP), theoretical CH4 pro-
duction (TMP) was calculated using Eqs. (1)–(2) (Wang et al., 2020c).
Feedstock carbon (C), hydrogen (H), and nitrogen (N) were tested by an
element analyzer (Vario EL cube, Elementar, Germany), while ash
content was measured gravimetrically by burning samples in a muffle
furnace. Oxygen (O) content was calculated by subtracting C, H, N, and
ash contents from 100 wt% (Lakshman et al., 2021). The EMP/TMP ratio
was calculated to represent the anaerobic biodegradability (%) of the
feedstock. All reactors were operated in duplicate, and average results
are reported.

2.2.3. Volatile fatty acids (VFAs) dynamics test
Sacrificial AD consisting of 175-mL plastic bottles were operated to
monitor the dynamics of VFA formation and consumption. Experimental
conditions and the ratio of feedstock, inoculum, water/nanobubble so-
lution, and headspace were the same as for the BMP test. Bottles were
manually swirled 2–3 times a day, and gas produced was vented every
1–2 days using a needle to penetrate the rubber septum. After thorough
mixing, three sacrificial bottles were removed for sampling every day for
the first 10 days. Subsequently, samples were taken on days 10, 11, 14,
and 30 from different bottles. Samples were centrifuged at 5000 rpm and
20 ◦C for 15 mins and the supernatant was filtered through 0.45 μm
membrane filters before analysis. The concentrations of VFAs, including
acetic acid, propionic acid, isobutyric acid, n-butyric acid, isovaleric
acid, and n-valeric acid, were measured by an ultra-fast liquid chro-
matograph (LC-30 CE, UFLC XR system, Shimadzu Ltd., Japan). An
equivalent methodology to that reported by (Baj´on Fern´andez et al.,
2015) was used with the exception of the HPLC run time, which was set
at 60 min. In Brief, the columns Biorad (cat. 125–0115) (105 mm × 7.8
mm) and the guard column (Biorad cat. 125–0131) were maintained at
65 ◦C. The injection volume was 15 μL and each sample was run for 0.5 h
using 1 mM sulphuric acid pumped at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. A
mixture of VFAs (acetic acid, propionic acid, butyric acid and valeric
acid analytical grade) at concentrations between 0.05 and 1 g/L was
used as internal standard.

3. Results and discussion
3.1. Nanobubble characterisations and stability
The generation of air nanobubbles was used to determine the optimal
pump operation duration for nanobubble solution preparation. After
operating the generator for 5 mins, the nanobubble concentration
reached 3.31 × 107 bubbles/mL, with a mean bubble diameter of 201
nm (Fig. 2a). Extending the pump operation time to 15 mins increased
the nanobubble concentration to 1.01 × 108 bubbles/mL (mean diam-
eter of 351.2 nm). However, further increasing the operation time to 20
mins resulted in stable nanobubble concentration and mean diameter,
maintaining values of 1.01 × 108 bubbles/mL and 354.3 nm, respec-
tively. The nanobubble concentrations (107–108 bubbles/mL) aligned
with findings from other studies using the same equipment (Lyu et al.,
2023; Wu et al., 2019, 2022), and were comparable to studies using
other hydraulic cavitation-based approaches (Table 1). The cavitation
process could release heat and elevate the temperature, potentially
acting as an external stimulator to induce nanobubble collapse and
hindering nanobubble formation. This could result in similar nano-
bubble generation when extending the operation time from 15 to 20
mins. The most effective operation time, determined to be 15 mins, was
used for preparing nanobubble solutions for the AD experiments.
The air nanobubble generation process can produce a supersaturated
solution with a DO level of 11–14 mg/L (Baram et al., 2022; Fan et al.,
2021), a phenomenon also confirmed in this study with an initial DO of
12.2 mg/L in the nanobubble solution. After leaving the solution at
ambient conditions (20–22 ◦C) for 72 h, the DO level decreased to 9.1
mg/L, comparable to the DO level in deionised water and equivalent to
the theoretical solubility of oxygen (8.9–9.2 mg/L). Both supersaturated
and non-supersaturated air nanobubble solutions were used in the AD
experiments to calculate the different contributions of dissolved gas and
nanobubbles.
The freshly prepared air, H2 and CO2 nanobubble solutions showed
similar nanobubble distribution, with a mean bubble size between 351.2
and 421.5 nm and concentrations of 9.88–10.2 × 107 bubbles/mL. The
nanobubble concentrations showed no significant changes after resting
at ambient conditions for 7 days (Fig. 2b). Some variations in D90 (90%
tile of bubble size) were observed (Fig. 2b), potentially attributed to the
nanobubble coalescence process (Chan et al., 2015). The results align
with previous studies indicating that nanobubbles can persist in water
from days to months (Zhang et al., 2018) due to the negative surface
charge associated stable bubble surface and reduced buoyance. The
exceptional stability of nanobubbles may contribute to long-term effects
in AD studies with operation durations extending several weeks.

3.2. Nanobubbles impact on biogas production
The cumulative production of both biogas (Fig. 3a) and CH4 (Fig. 3b)
increased rapidly until day 7, reducing the rate of production thereafter
until the reactors were stopped on day 30. In the CO2, Air and H2
nanobubble treatment groups, the cumulative biogas yield reached
643.3, 569.5, and 521.9 mL/g VSadded, respectively, showcasing im-
provements of 33.3 %, 18 %, and 8.1 % compared to the control group.
Similar to previous studies (Wang et al., 2019; Yang et al., 2019),
nanobubble treatment did not alter the CH4 concentration (49 %–54 %
after day 7) in any of the groups. Compared to the control group, the CH4
production increments followed the same order: 37.1 % (372.4 mL/g
VS), 25.6 % (340.9 mL/g VS), and 14.5 % (310.9 mL/g VS) in the CO2,
Air and H2 nanobubble treatment groups, respectively.
The anaerobic biodegradability of the feedstock sludge, defined as
EMP/TMP ratio, increased from 33.8 % in the control group to
38.7–46.4 % for all nanobubble treatments (Table 2). This was theorised
to be attributed to the nanobubble-induced generated ROS, increased
water mobility and improved microbial electron transport activity
(Chuenchart et al., 2021; Zhu et al., 2024). ROS can theoretically in-
crease water mobility by disrupting hydrogen bonds and enhancing
solubilisation of organic matter, which improves microbial electron
transport activity by stimulating enzyme functions and optimising the
electron transport chain. These effects lead to an upregulation of the
EMP pathway and an increase in the EMP/TMP ratio, indicating
enhanced metabolic efficiency and methane production in the AD pro-
cess. The more biodegradable sludge resulted in enhanced CH4 pro-
duction (14.5 %–37.1 %) compared to the control group, which aligns
with reported values (0.59–39.3 %) from previous studies conducting in-
situ nanobubble treatment (Table 1). The current study focused on
sludge that had been pretreated on site with thermal-hydrolysis, which
is expected to have reduced the contribution of nanobubbles to further
enhance hydrolysis (Wang et al., 2020c), explaining the higher biogas
uplifts observed for CO2 than air nanobubbles as CO2 can act as a sub-
strate in intermediate reactions of the AD process. Injection of CO2
nanobubbles resulted in the greatest positive impact on biogas and CH4
generation, which has been previously attributed to an increased
homoacetogenic activity promoting acetoclastic methanogenesis (Baj´on
Fern´andez et al., 2019; Muntau et al., 2021) or to a boost on hydro-
genotrophic methanogenesis (Fan et al., 2020a, 2020b). Interestingly,
H2 nanobubble treatment resulted in lower improvements in biogas and
CH4 generation than seen for CO2 and air nanobubbles, although the
increase (14.5 %) was within the range (6.7–39.3 %) reported in pre-
vious studies (Fan et al., 2020a; Wang et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2024). It is
postulated that the reduced benefit of H2 injection compared with air or
CO2 could be linked to a lack of sufficient electron acceptors on the
solution or to a mild inhibition resulting from hydrogen supersaturation
exceeding thermodynamic limits for acetogenic bacteria (Wainaina
et al., 2019; Rocamora et al., 2023).
Addition of air nanobubble solutions with and without supersatu-
rated dissolved O2 was conducted to quantify the relative contributions
to biogas uplifts of increased O2 dissolved levels and the nanobubbles
themselves. The biogas and CH4 productions were only slightly higher
(569.5 and 340.9 mL/g VSadded, respectively) in the supersaturated air

3.3. CH4 production kinetics
The Modified Gompertz model and the first-order kinetics model are
commonly used to describe CH4 production kinetics in AD (Ponzelli
et al., 2022). In this study, both models effectively simulated (R2 > 0.97)
the CH4 production dynamics from all groups (Table 2), supporting their
applicability in predicting CH4 production from AD of sewage sludge
under nanobubble technology intensification. The k value of the first-
order model is an important parameter directly reflecting the rate of
hydrolysis, with a higher value indicating faster substrate hydrolysis. As
the feedstock sludge in this study underwent thermal-hydrolysis pre-
treatment, the k values (0.14–0.23 d−1) from nanobubble treatment
groups were not higher than for the control group (0.21 d−1). The results
suggest that nanobubble technology is able to stimulate CH4 production
by contributing to acidogenic and methanogenic processes.
Similar to the results of cumulative CH4 production (Fig. 3b), the
maximum CH4 production rate (Rmax) obtained from the Modified
Gompertz model was highest in CO2 nanobubble treatment group
(349.35 mL/gVS/d) with a 35.9 % increase compared to the control
group (257.1 mL/gVS/d). The lag time (λ) is a parameter that indicates
the initial adaptability of microbes to the AD system, where a lower λ
value is desirable for a rapid biogas formation. The λ values in all groups
were relatively low (λ < 0.97 day), which may be attributed to the
feedstock and inoculum used for the laboratory trials being sourced from
the same site, so the microbial communities would have been adapted to
the substrate material. For large-scale AD operations, lower lag time
offers increased efficiency, consistent biogas supply, and optimised
reactor design.
3.4. VFAs dynamics
VFAs are intermediates produced during the acidogenesis and ace-
togenesis stages of AD through the transformation of easily biodegrad-
able organic matter after the hydrolysis phase (Chuenchart et al., 2021).
Attributed to thermal-hydrolysis pretreatment, the initial VFA concen-
trations (1000–1700 mg/L) in this study were significantly higher than
those (150–750 mg/L) observed in other AD studies involving waste
activated sludge (WAS) and its co-digestion with lignocellulosic biomass
(Yang et al., 2019, 2020).
The higher accumulation of VFAs recorded in all nanobubble injec-
ted ADs over the first week of the trial, when compared with the control
reactors, suggests that nanobubbles had a role in further enhancing
hydrolysis, potentially attributed to the generated ROS, even if the
material was subjected to thermal treatment on-site. There is previous
evidence of thermal pre-treatment hydrolysing macromolecules but also
increasing solubility of others that then remain solubilised but unhy-
drolyzed (Wilson and Novak, 2009). The increased VFA levels on the test
reactors (Fig. 4) could result from nanobubbles supporting hydrolysis of
those previously solubilised macromolecules, a hypothesis that merits
further investigation. The changes of VFA generally followed CH4 pro-
duction (Fig. 2b), with the highest accumulation observed in the CO2
nanobubble treatment group, followed by air (supersaturated), H2, and
air (non-supersaturated) nanobubble treatment groups. The results were
distinct to previous AD studies focused on other non-pre-treated feed-
stock, as air/O2 nanobubble additions are normally deemed as better
treatment to induce higher biodegradability of sludge and VFA pro-
duction due to higher oxygenation capacity of the organic substrates. In
this study, therefore, CO2 nanobubble addition improved acidification
during digestion, leading to the highest enhanced VFA production
(Chuenchart et al., 2021). It is postulated that the reduced benefit of H2
injection compared with air or CO2 could be linked to a lack of sufficient
electron acceptors on the solution or to a mild inhibition resulting from
hydrogen supersaturation exceeding thermodynamic limits for aceto-
genic bacteria (Wainaina et al., 2019; Rocamora et al., 2023).
Further analysis of VFA species revealed a higher increment in acetic
acid and propionic acid, with no obvious differences in the proportions
of isobutyric, n-butyric and isovaleric acids. VFA concentrations in all
groups showed a sharp reduction after the initial acidification (Fig. 4), as
expected on batch trials where feedstock supply is limited. In summary,
the results support that nanobubble treatment may stimulate microbial
communities and enzyme activities in thermally pre-treated AD, offering
a tailored approach for AD intensification.
4. Conclusions and future perspectives
This study demonstrated that the specific properties of nanobubbles,
rather than supersaturated gas, serves as the primary driver in
enhancing AD performance with nanobubble technology. Nanobubbles
played a crucial role in increasing feedstock biodegradability and CH4
production. Moreover, CO2 nanobubbles, compared to air and H2
nanobubbles, were most effective in enhancing acidogenesis/aceto-
genesis for VFA production and methanogenesis for CH4 yield. The
following efforts are recommended towards technology development
and implementation:
1) All previous and current studies were conducted in batch AD sys-
tems, further efforts are urged to carry out a demonstration study in a
long-term continuous reactor.
2) Different gases have shown specific functions in AD intensification,
thus, tailored operational strategies, such as multiple stages of
