ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the integration between two technologies, a demonstration-scale desorption chamber (DC) and microaeration, in a demonstration-
scale UASB reactor treating real domestic sewage, aiming to enhance methane recovery and reduce hydrogen sulfide. The DC removes dissolved CH₄ and 
H2S from liquid, generating a waste gas (containing CH₄, CO₂, H2S, and air - O₂ and N2), which was used in this study to microaerate the UASB reactor. The DC 
achieved median removal efficiencies of 55% for dissolved methane and 79% for dissolved hydrogen sulfide, despite the low gas-to-liquid ratio applied (rQ 
≈ 0.042). Waste gas extracted from the DC was successfully reused as the microaeration source in the UASB reactor, resulting in a median 28% reduction 
in H₂S mass flow and a 60% decrease in biogas H₂S concentration, while also increasing methane mass flow by approximately 37%, even though the doses 
of O₂ and operational conditions varied substantially throughout the study. The integration of a desorption chamber and a microaerated UASB reactor 
produced biogas with a typical composition of ~50–54% CH₄ and ~800 ppmv H₂S, suitable for thermal energy recovery without any additional treatment 
or reducing the required treatment for energy recovery (biogas generator). The demonstration-scale conditions highlighted both the technical feasibility 
of the approach and the operational challenges of controlling microaeration under real wastewater variability, indicating a need for further research to 
standardize dosing strategies (L O2/L biogas instead of L O2/L effluent) to support full-scale implementation.
Keywords: waste gas; biogas; energy recovery; odor nuisance; sulfide oxidation.

INTRODUCTION
Anaerobic reactors, particularly the Upflow Anaerobic Sludge Blanket (UASB) 
type, are widely used for sewage treatment in Brazil due to their operational 
simplicity, low sludge production, low energy consumption, and biogas genera-
tion, among other advantages. However, they also present limitations, including 
the need for post-treatment and challenges in managing biogas and dissolved 
gases, particularly methane (CH₄), a potent greenhouse gas, and hydrogen 
sulfide (H₂S), which is associated with odor, corrosion, and toxicity (Bressani-
Ribeiro and Chernicharo, 2024).
Dissolved methane (CH₄-D) has become a central topic in discussions 
regarding greenhouse gas emissions from wastewater treatment centers, espe-
cially in anaerobic systems. Unlike the methane recovered in the gaseous phase, 
this fraction remains in the liquid effluent. It may be gradually released into the 
atmosphere through desorption during transport or in downstream units within 
the WWTP. This release can increase overall emissions, as CH₄-D may account 
for 40% of the methane produced during treatment (Souza, Chernicharo and 
Aquino, 2011; Souza, Chernicharo and Melo, 2012). According to the IPCC 
(2019), methane emissions in anaerobic reactors are typically estimated based 
on the removed organic load, the maximum methane production potential 
(Bo), and the methane conversion factor (MCF), assuming that all methane 
is emitted as biogas. In this context, unaccounted CH₄-D may represent a sig-
nificant source of fugitive emissions, especially when no removal, capture, or 
recovery strategies are in place, potentially affecting the facility’s GHG balance 
and decreasing the system’s energy efficiency.
Hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) is also a particularly problematic dissolved gas., A 
colorless, toxic, and corrosive compound, denser than air and partially soluble 
in water, it is characterized by a “rotten egg” odor and detectable by the human 
olfactory system at extremely low concentrations, on the order of 0.00047 ppmv 
(Metcalf and Eddy, 2016). As a result, it is a major contributor to operational 
issues in wastewater treatment plants, including odor nuisance, corrosion of

dominantly present in the dissolved phase, accounting for 75.1% of the sulfide 
generated and corresponding to 57–64% of the influent sulfur. In contrast, the 
fraction present in the biogas is comparatively small, representing only 1.3% 
of the hydrogen sulfide formed and 0.9% of the influent sulfur (Souza, 2010; 
Souza, Chernicharo and Melo, 2012).
One technique used to mitigate these issues is the desorption chamber (DC), 
which removes dissolved gases from anaerobic effluents (Glória et al., 2016; 
Huete et al., 2018). In this process, a large mass of CH₄ and H₂S is desorbed 
from the liquid phase and/or partially oxidized, generating a secondary gas 
stream, referred to as waste gas, which also contains significant amounts of 
air (N₂ and O₂).
In this study, this waste gas, typically a residual stream requiring treatment, 
was repurposed as a source of microaeration for the UASB reactor, reintro-
ducing the dissolved gases removed from the system. Microaeration, defined 
as the controlled introduction of small amounts of oxygen, has emerged as 
a promising strategy to enhance anaerobic reactor performance (Castro, 
Azevedo and Souza, 2021; Fu et al., 2023), as it can improve methane yield, 
reduce H₂S concentrations, and stabilize the process. However, challenges 
such as precise oxygen dosing, lack of standardization, and limited opera-
tional guidance still hinder large-scale implementation (Fu et al., 2023). 
Due to these challenges and to support the interpretation of some results of 
this study, one goal is to explore a more stable and efficiency-based param-
eter for microaeration.
Another aspect is that the research on microaeration has largely focused 
on sludge and high-strength waste digesters, mainly at a laboratory or pilot 
scale. At the same time, few studies have explored its application to domes-
tic sewage, particularly in full-scale UASB systems (Chen et al., 2020). In this 
context, the current study evaluated the integration of a desorption chamber 
with microaeration by applying the waste gas stream in a UASB reactor treat-
ing real domestic sewage, aiming to improve energy recovery, enhance system 
performance, and environmental outcomes.

The UASB reactor, which had been in operation for ten years prior to the 
start of the experiment, was fed with approximately 1.48 m³ h⁻¹ of domestic 
wastewater, resulting in a hydraulic retention time (HRT) of approximately 
9.5 h and volumetric organic loading rate of 0.96 ± 0.55 kg COD m-3 day-1. 
The desorption chamber received the entire effluent from the UASB reactor, 
ensuring full system integration. In this unit, the average hydraulic loading rate 
(HLR) was 0.26 ± 0.04 m³ m⁻² min⁻¹, corresponding to applied flow rates of 
0.41 ± 0.05 L s⁻¹. Gas extraction from the desorption chamber was performed 
using a Masterflex peristaltic pump with variable speed control (0–600 rpm).
To evaluate the different microaeration application points and compare 
them with the conventional UASB reactor, three main phases were established. 
The study began with a control anaerobic phase (F0), during which the desorp-
tion chamber was monitored and adjusted for the subsequent use of waste gas 
for microaeration. This phase was characterized by low gas flow rates and, con-
sequently, low gas-to-effluent flow ratios (rQ), with an average value of 0.042 ± 
0.010. After this initial period, Phase F0 was applied at shorter intervals between 
transitions or whenever microaeration was interrupted due to unit maintenance 
(Figure 2). Consequently, the control anaerobic phase, later used as a reference 
for comparison with the microaerated reactor, was distributed chronologically 
throughout the entire experiment, resulting in mean values that were more rep-
resentative and less influenced by seasonal factors (rainy periods, temperature 
variations, and water consumption habits).
Subsequently, Phases 1 and 2 were implemented. These experimental phases 
involved evaluating different microaeration injection points within the UASB 
reactor. Two points of application were tested: in the headspace of the three-phase 
separator, approximately in the middle, and submerged in the water column 
of the three-phase separator, at a level between 0.5 and 1,0 m below the water 
surface. The experiment was divided into three experimental phases, as shown 
in Table 2. The desorption chamber observation followed the same microaera-
tion phases to simplify analysis and comparisons. The experiment timeline can 
be found in Figure 2, focusing on the phase-change period.
System performance monitoring encompassed a range of physical-chem-
ical and gas-related parameters, using standard methods and instrumenta-
tion. Influent and effluent flow rates were measured directly in the field. 
Dissolved methane concentrations were determined using the headspace 
extraction method followed by gas chromatography, as described by Souza, 
Chernicharo and Aquino (2011). Dissolved hydrogen sulfide was quantified 
by the colorimetric method, according to Plas et al. (1992) and pH, temper-
ature, and dissolved oxygen were monitored with a multiparameter probe 
(HACH® HQ40D). Biogas production was quantified using a gas meter 
(Ritter® TG3), while biogas composition required a portable gas analyzer 
equipped with electrochemical sensors (LANDTEC® GEM™ 5000). Statistical 
analyses were conducted using the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test, 
with a 5% significance level.
For the calculation of mass flow, flow rate data were normalized. A calcu-
lation of the biogas generated in the UASB reactor was carried out to estimate 
hydrogen sulfide removal efficiency and the increase in methane mass flow in 
the microaerated UASB reactor. The proper flow of biogas was calculated by 
subtracting the waste gas injected into the reactor from the total biogas with 
microaeration, while the composition was estimated from the average biogas 
composition obtained during the control anaerobic phase (F0).
After the initial data analysis, an additional evaluation was conducted to 
better correlate the microaeration dosage with the observed process efficiencies.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION
Desorption Chamber Performance
The experimental period in the desorption chamber lasted approximately 
540 days, comprising phases with and without microaeration integration. 
Figure 3 presents the main results, with vertical dashed lines indicating the 
onset of microaeration (phases 1 and 2). The median removal efficiencies of 
dissolved methane and hydrogen sulfide were 55% and 79%, respectively, with 
maximum values of 78% and 99%, respectively. 
These medians were lower than those reported in previous studies: Glória 
et al. (2016) achieved a 60% methane and 84% hydrogen sulfide removal at a 
gas-to-liquid ratio (rQ) of 3.1, Huete et al. (2017) achieved methane removal 
efficiencies of up to 99% with rQ values between 0.5 and 1.0, while Santo et al. 
(2019) reported 89% and 86% removals, respectively, with an rQ of 0.1. In con-
trast, Santo et al. (2019) also used a rQ of 0.05 in a very similar desorption 
chamber, achieving 43% methane and 54% hydrogen sulfide removal, indicat-
ing that rQ plays an important role in removal effectiveness. 
The relatively lower efficiencies in the current study can be attributed to a 
group of aspects such as: 
(1)	 the low average rQ applied (0.042), less than half the value used by Santo 
et al. (2019), which limited gas renewal at the interfaces and, consequently, 
mass transfer; 
(2)	 the variability of the real domestic wastewater; 
(3)	 higher hydraulic loading rate compared to Glória et al. (2016).
As illustrated in Figure 3b, the removal efficiencies of dissolved CH₄ and 
H₂S (%) and the methane concentration in the desorption chamber gas (% v/v) 
fluctuated throughout the experimental period. Regarding the methane con-
centration, values generally range from 20 to 30%.
Despite a few operational changes in the desorption chamber, establishing 
experimental phases was crucial to verify external influences and the relation 
between the DC and microaeration results. When the experimental phases 
are analyzed individually, clearer trends can be observed in the removal effi-
ciencies of dissolved methane (CH₄) and dissolved hydrogen sulfide (H₂S) 
across the experimental phases (F0, F1, and F2), as presented in Figure 4. 
For CH₄ (Figure 4a), removal efficiencies predominantly ranged from 50 to 
60%, with medians around 54 to 59% for all phases. Phases F0 and F1 exhib-
ited lower data dispersion, indicating a more stable system behavior. In con-
trast, phase F2 showed greater variability, with minimum values near 20%, 
suggesting operational instability or a stronger influence of environmental 
factors on process performance.
Regarding H₂S (Figure 4b), removal efficiencies were higher than those 
observed for CH₄, with medians of 75–80%. However, dispersion was more

The removal efficiencies obtained were lower than those reported in 
other studies. In review studies of air-based microaeration, Krayzelova et al. 
(2015) reported removal efficiencies consistently above 72%, and Chen et al. 
(2020) observed a minimum efficiency of 52% in diverse anaerobic reactors. 
Castro, Azevedo and Souza (2021) concluded that air addition in the diges-
tion zone of a UASB reactor treating domestic sewage showed hydrogen sul-
fide removal efficiencies from biogas greater than 90%, and Kraakman et at. 
(2023) reported removal efficiencies of at least 80% for H2S in large-scale, air 
microaerated wastewater municipal sludge digester biogas.
An important factor contributing to this lower efficiency is the gas used in 
microaeration, which has a lower oxygen concentration than air (approximately 
10%). In addition, the waste gas from the DC presents median hydrogen sulfide 
concentrations of around 190 ppmv (F0), 500 ppmv (F1), and 380 ppmv (F2), 
which also contribute to the H₂S content in the final biogas.  Possibly the most 
important factor behind the understanding of the removal of H₂S around 30% 
is the residence time of the biogas in the headspace. This number accounted 
for a relatively low value of about 0.40 h, on average. Krayzelova et al. (2015), 
based on other studies, reported removal efficiencies that vary with residence 
time in the headspace, with removals of around 90% for residence times of 3–5 h 
and about 40% for residence times of approximately 1 h. However, achieving 
such high residence times in typical UASB reactors could result in substantial 
changes to the design parameters of the three-phase separators and an increase 
in associated costs.
On the other hand, the approximately 28% efficiency represents an inter-
esting, cost-effective removal, although relatively low, as the resulting biogas 
presented median concentrations of 820 ppmv. Compared to the typical H₂S 
concentration in biogas (2000 ppmv), the concentration reduction was achieved 
around 60%. Such a concentration (820 ppmv) directly meets the quality require-
ments for thermal energy recovery in boilers, without the need for additional 
treatment (< 1000 ppmv; Wellinger and Lindberg, 2001).
As seen in Figure 7b, the relative increase in CH₄ mass flow (g d⁻¹) in the 
biogas was similar across operational phases F1 and F2, with a median of approx-
imately 37% and considerable variability in the data. Statistical analysis indi-
cated no significant differences between the two phases at the 5% significance 
level (Mann–Whitney test). Therefore, submerging the injection point for the 
DC waste gas did not provide an observable advantage in methane recovery.
The increase in methane mass flow was estimated through a mass balance 
approach. The total CH₄ mass flow measured in the mixed gas stream was 
compared with the expected CH₄ mass flow assuming that no DC waste gas 
was introduced. This reference condition was calculated considering the nor-
malized total volumetric flow minus the normalized volumetric flow of waste 
gas, and assuming that the CH₄ concentration in the resulting flow would cor-
respond to the average CH₄ concentration measured in the anaerobic phase.
Based on this calculation procedure, the observed increase in CH₄ mass 
flow is likely associated with the recovery of dissolved methane by the DC unit. 
In contrast, the possibility that microaeration occurring in the upper region 
of the three-phase separator significantly enhanced anaerobic biodegradation 
is considered low (Castro, Azevedo and Souza, 2021). Previous studies have 
shown that dissolved methane losses in the effluent of UASB reactors may 
reach 60% of the methane usually captured in the biogas (Souza, Chernicharo 
and Aquino, 2011).
From an energy recovery perspective, the 37% increase represents a poten-
tially valuable gain in methane. Although some dilution of methane occurred due 
to mixing between biogas and DC waste gas, the resulting CH₄ concentration 
was around 54%, indicating that the dilution was not pronounced. The waste 
gas stream still contained relevant concentrations of recovered CH₄ and CO₂, 
approximately 22 and 5%, respectively. Moreover, methane concentrations 
around 54% remain suitable for most thermal and electrical energy conversion 
systems operating with biogas (Sun et al., 2015; Wellinger and Lindberg, 2001).
Concerning the point of oxygen/gas application, submerged microaera-
tion presented disadvantages compared to headspace injection. These included 
a trend toward lower removal efficiency, likely due to oxygen consumption in 
the liquid phase, as well as operational challenges (e.g., diffuser inspection and 
replacement) and increased system complexity. Supporting the preference for 
headspace application, most sulfur-oxidizing bacteria (SOB) are predominantly 
found in the headspace, indicating that sulfide oxidation is primarily occurring 
there (Krayzelova et al., 2015).

Oxygen and methane concentrations for  
operational safety of the UASB/DC system
To discuss safety aspects related to microaerated biogas in the UASB reactor and 
to waste gas in the DC, the respective graphs (Figures 10a and 10b) show oxy-
gen and methane concentrations over the experimental period (time counted 
from the onset of microaeration using waste gas, Phase 1). The operating con-
ditions of the gas lines (biogas and waste gas) were: ambient temperature (aver-
age around 25°C) and pressure around atmospheric pressure (1 atm); therefore, 
the safety parameters used below refer to conditions close to these standards, 
obtained from the flammability diagram between CH4, O2, and N2.
In the microaerated UASB reactor (Figure 10a), O₂ concentrations always 
remained below 8%, well under the minimum oxygen concentration (MOC) 
of 12%. This value represents the threshold which combustion cannot occur 
due to the lack of oxidant/oxygen. Additionally, CH₄ concentrations were much 
higher than the upper flammability limit (UFL) of 15% in air, a value above 
which combustion cannot occur due to excess fuel/methane (Crowl, 2023; Guo 
et al., 2024; Mashuga and Crowl, 1998).
For the waste gas in the DC under operation (Figure 10b), O₂ concentra-
tions were expectedly higher, but with median and mean values close to 10%, 
and thus frequently (over 75% of the data) below the MOC of 12%. O₂ concen-
trations approached 12% on only three days, and on two additional days they 
presented values of approximately 13–14%. On the other hand, even when con-
centrations were close to or slightly above the MOC (12–14%), the minimum 
CH₄ concentration required for the mixture to be flammable would be within 
much more restrictive and reduced ranges, approximately 6–10% CH₄ (Crowl, 
2003; Mashuga and Crowl, 1998). Methane concentrations in the waste gas were 
always above 16%, with mean and median values close to 24%.
The 12% MOC value is specifically applied to contexts of methane dilution 
with nitrogen (N2) and air. When CO2 is present, this limit can rise to 14.5% 
(Crowl, 2003), making the safety range less restrictive. In the current study, bio-
gas and waste gas had average CO2 concentrations of 6 and 5%, respectively, 
along with high N2 levels of 35 and 61%, respectively. Due to this composi-
tion, the 12% O2 limit is a more conservative and therefore safer approach. 
Additionally, an extra safety margin is recommended, establishing the opera-
tional limit at 10% O2 (Crowl, 2003). Therefore, in future studies and appli-
cations, it is recommended to adjust the microaeration rate considering this 
oxygen limit (10%) in the waste gas as well.

CONCLUSION
The results of this study demonstrate that integrating a desorption chamber with 
microaeration into a UASB reactor is a promising strategy to mitigate dissolved 
gases, achieving relevant removals of CH₄ and H₂S with low energy demand 
and simple operation. The use of waste gas as a source of microaeration proved 
technically feasible. Regarding the injection point, no major differences were 
observed between the headspace and submerged applications. 
This study achieved performance close to, and even exceeding, a 37% increase 
in CH4 in the biogas mass flow and a 28% H2S removal in the mixed biogas and 
waste gas mass flows. As a characterization of the mixed and treated flow, it was 
possible to reach concentrations of approximately 50% CH4 and 800 ppm H2S, 
which can be used in various contexts as renewable energy.
As this study was conducted in a demonstration-scale unit treating real 
domestic wastewater under practical conditions, the findings provide valu-
able insights into the limitations and the potential of this integrated approach. 
Nevertheless, challenges remain with oxygen dosing control and the opera-
tional variability inherent to full-scale plants, including fluctuations in influ-
ent characteristics, flow rate, and biogas production. These results highlight 
the need for further investigation to establish robust operational guidelines 
and to advance the scalability of microaeration in anaerobic sewage treatment 
systems. Future studies should also evaluate alternative control parameters for 
microaeration, such as the L O₂/L biogas ratio.
